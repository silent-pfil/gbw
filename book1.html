Prologue — Veil of Green

Dr. Elara Voss had stopped counting the hours since her last real sleep somewhere around the fourth Green Shift. She'd stopped counting the Green Shifts themselves somewhere around the seventh. What she hadn't stopped doing, not once in four years, was hoping that the next one would be different.

The observation deck of the Horizon Echo held its usual hush — the particular quiet of a room built for waiting. Beyond the curved viewport, Epsilon-9-4 turned in slow, patient orbit: a turquoise jewel wrapped in cloud, oceans catching what little starlight reached this far out, continents green with something spectrography swore, again and again, was chlorophyll. It was, by every instrument aboard, a perfect world. The kind that should have hummed with radio chatter. The kind that should have argued with itself in a hundred languages, launched crude satellites, made all the beautiful, noisy mistakes of a species discovering it wasn't alone in its own atmosphere.

"Planet Epsilon-9-4," SensorAI offered, its tone carrying the same bright, faintly maddening cheer it always did at this hour. "Surface pressure, 1.03 atmospheres. Composition seventy-eight percent nitrogen, twenty percent oxygen, trace carbon dioxide — breathable, if you didn't mind the ghosts already living in it. Three major oceans, forty-one percent land coverage, weather patterns consistent with a temperate, seasonal world, poles unfrozen. Biosignatures consistent with complex multicellular life — chlorophyll analogs across the visible band, methane fluctuations consistent with large-scale respiration, atmospheric trace chemistry consistent with an industrial era tapering into something cleaner. No orbital debris detected. No satellite constellations. No broadcast chatter on any radio or laser band we know to listen on — the one exception being a single, faint, rhythmically timed pulse in the microwave band, cosmic-sonar in character. Likely wayfinding. Projected technological signature… moderate, trending toward significant."

(Elara had never once asked why the ship's subsystems all carried the same flat, unimaginative naming scheme — SensorAI, NavigationAI, CommunicationAI, MaintenanceAI — while the same man who'd named them had spent an embarrassing number of hours arguing with a certification board over what to call the ship's cat. Mateo's affection, it turned out, was not evenly distributed.)

Elara didn't answer right away. She'd learned, over the long, ion-slow years of this survey — the gravity assists, the patient arcs between stars that took the better part of a human life to complete — that the silence right here, right before the numbers turned, was worth holding onto. It was the last silence that still had hope folded into it.

"Here we go," Mateo murmured, not looking up.

On the main display, the numbers began their slide.

"Green Shift initiating," SensorAI reported, its cheer dimming, just slightly, in a way none of them had ever been able to explain or reprogram out of it. "Gravitational signature softening. That faint sonar pulse — the closest thing to noise this world ever made — has fallen below detectable threshold. Whatever's left down there isn't reaching for it anymore. Biosignature curve inflecting downward. EM emissions spiking, briefly, in a band we don't have a name for yet."

Elara had watched this seven times before. She'd told herself, each time, that watching it an eighth would feel like routine. It never did. The planet didn't die. It didn't erupt, didn't crack, didn't do any of the violent things a species half-expects the universe to do when something impossible happens to a world. It simply, slowly, veiled itself — the turquoise fading by degrees, the biosignatures flattening into background noise, the gravitational anomaly that had screamed inhabited for months softening into something a lazier survey would have logged as empty rock and moved on from. Within hours, Epsilon-9-4 would read as just another quiet, lifeless Goldilocks world. Beautiful. Untouched. Alone.

"Why do they keep disappearing on us?" Elara asked the glass, or the planet, or no one at all.

Mateo Okoye didn't look up from the panel he was only pretending to still be working on. "Because the universe hates us specifically," he said. "Every single time we find a living world, it ghosts us before the second date. We are the cosmic equivalent of showing up to the party exactly as the last light goes out."

It got a laugh — small, tired, the kind that comes out more from muscle memory than actual humor. Four years of this had worn most of the genuine laughter down to its outline. They'd found life seven times and lost it seven times, and somewhere in there the whole crew had quietly stopped talking about what that meant, because none of the available answers were ones a person could carry comfortably for very long.

Elara's hand found the small badge pinned at her collar, the way it always did without her quite deciding to. Leaf-shaped, faintly iridescent even in the dim deck lighting — a relic of a stranger, hopeful century, when humanity still believed it might photosynthesize its way to the stars on nothing but sunlight and its own stubborn optimism. Her grandmother's badge. Passed down through hands that had believed a great many things nobody else in the family could ever quite explain.

"Log it," Elara said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Another Green Shift. Another lost hope."

She meant it. She also, somewhere she wasn't ready to look at directly, didn't believe it. Not the lost part. She never quite could.

⁂

Light-years away — a distance that meant something different depending on who was doing the measuring — something in the dark between the stars had already begun to stir.

A vast membrane unfolded, slow and certain, like aurora deciding to become something more solid than light. It was not a ship, not exactly, though it moved the way a ship might if a ship had never needed fuel or hull or any of the small, brave compromises a species makes when it hasn't yet learned to stop needing things. Deep within it, gauges that were not gauges flickered in a dim, unhurried rhythm, keeping time with something far older than any clock humanity had built.

A figure watched the turquoise world complete its quiet transformation — calm, faintly green at the edges, unmistakably human in the shape it had chosen to keep, the way a person keeps an old coat long after they've stopped needing the warmth. He had watched this exact moment more times than he could easily count anymore, though count had stopped meaning quite what it once did.

Kael Ren allowed himself something close to a smile.

"Not lost," he said, to the vessel that was, in every way that mattered, also a part of him. "Just… stepping forward."

The membrane drank in starlight without hurry — faint radio whispers, the subtlest gradients of the cosmic background, the accumulated hush of a world that had just stopped shouting. Another civilization had chosen coherence over collapse. Another small, stubborn spark of intelligent life had looked at the edge of everything and decided, together, to become something larger than the sum of what it used to be.

And far across a gulf that words like distance only barely described, on a small, loud, blue planet its own people called Earth, a new signal — faint, half-formed, easy to miss if you weren't listening exactly the right way — was, for the first time in a very long while, about to be answered.

The Green Shift had never been a disappearance.

It had always, only ever, been an invitation.

The trouble, as it so often is with invitations, was getting anyone to notice it had arrived at all.

Chapter One — The Odometer

The Vigil looked the same as it had every morning for as long as Elara could remember, which was itself a kind of lie, because nothing about it had ever actually stayed the same. It only felt that way. That was the point of ritual, she supposed — to make the changing feel like standing still.

She sat cross-legged on the low cushion in the corner of her quarters, knees tucked, palms resting open on her thighs, eyes closed against the soft predawn dim the room's lighting had been coaxed into mimicking. To her left, low on a shelf built into the bulkhead: the instruments. A cracked slide rule that had belonged to someone five generations back, a data crystal no reader on the ship could parse anymore, a single sealed vial of something her ancestor had called, in careful handwriting on a yellowed label, first successful yield. Science. To her right, closer to the bed: photographs, actual printed photographs, curling gently at the corners no matter the climate control. Faces going back further than the ship's own service record. Family.

Centered in front of her, on its own small stand, sat the only object in the room that traveled with her every single day without fail. A badge, leaf-shaped, faintly iridescent even in the dim predawn light — her grandmother's, and her grandmother's grandmother's before that, passed down through hands that had believed a great many things nobody else in the family could ever quite explain. She rested it there each night, at the exact center of the Vigil, and lifted it again each morning, the way you might return a question to its proper place and then pick it back up to keep carrying it.

She breathed. She let the ship's low hum settle into the space behind her ribs the way it always did, indistinguishable after enough years from her own pulse. She was, for these few minutes, entirely and uncomplicatedly pleased with her life, in a way she never quite managed to be for the rest of the day.

The door chimed once, then opened without waiting for an answer, because it never did.

"Happy mili-light-year-travel-day," Mateo said, leaning against the frame with the specific, punchable cheer of a man who had clearly practiced the line in the mirror.

"That's not a thing." She didn't open her eyes.

"It's absolutely a thing. I looked it up. Well — I made it up, and then I looked at it, and it held up under scrutiny, so functionally, it's a thing."

"Mateo."

"There's cake."

That got one eye open.

"Real cake?"

"There is flour-adjacent material shaped like cake, iced with something Reggie assures me is food, and yes, there are candles, and yes, I already lit one wrong and now there's a small fire situation that Eliza is handling." He said the last word with the particular emphasis of a man relaying a message he'd been given verbatim and didn't fully trust. "Mess hall. Ten minutes. Everyone's already there, which is the same as saying everyone was already there twenty minutes ago and has been getting progressively bored and more annoying about it."

She let the moment finish on its own terms before she rose — a small mercy she allowed herself, even now, even for something as small as a birthday. She lifted the badge from its place at the Vigil's center and pinned it to her collar, the way she did every morning, a habit so old it barely registered as a choice anymore. It didn't warm under her fingers. It never did. It simply sat there, patient and unbothered, the way it always had.

⁂

The mess hall smelled like burnt sugar and someone's overconfident attempt at frosting, and the small fire situation had, true to report, been handled, though a faint char mark on the counter would apparently be a permanent addition to the ship's decor. Mateo had, of course, gone with the strangest candle number he could justify — not her actual age, which would have required a conversation none of them particularly wanted to have about how many of those years she'd spent doing exactly this — but her cosmic odometer instead. A little over four hundredths of a light-year, if you wanted to be exact about it, which Mateo very much did.

"Zero point oh-three light-years," he announced, gesturing at the cake like a man unveiling a monument. "Give or take. You've officially traveled the distance from here to almost nowhere, Dr. Voss, and I think that deserves recognition."

"It's the same birthday I've had my whole life, Mateo."

"It's the same birthday everyone's had their whole life. That's not the point. The point is we finally have a unit of measurement that isn't just how many times has this rock gone around that other rock, which, frankly, always struck me as a little provincial."

"Make a wish," someone called from the back, and she did, mostly out of the same reflexive courtesy that made her thank machines for holding doors.

She'd barely opened her eyes again when the air in the room changed.

It wasn't dramatic, not at first — no flare of light, no groan of strained metal, nothing that would have registered on any sensor as an event. It was more like the room had quietly decided to hold its breath. The overhead lighting didn't dim so much as it seemed, briefly, embarrassed by its own brightness. And then, standing near the head of the table where no one had been standing a half-second before, there was a shape.

It was green — unmistakably, deliberately green, the exact shade of the badge pinned at her own collar — and it was, just as unmistakably, not entirely there. She could see the edge of the counter faintly through the outline of his shoulder. His feet, if he had feet in any sense that mattered, didn't quite meet the floor. He looked, more than anything, like a very good projection of a man who had never needed to learn what a projection was.

Nobody screamed. Nobody moved. The cake, still smoking faintly at one corner, sat between them all like the world's least appropriate offering to whatever this was.

"Hello," the shape said, and his voice arrived a half-beat out of sync with his mouth, the way distant thunder arrives after lightning that's already finished happening. "I hope I'm not early."

Mateo, to his eternal credit, was the first to find words. "You are extremely early to a conversation we have not had yet."

The shape — the man, if man still applied — turned, and looked directly at Elara with an expression she would spend a very long time afterward trying to describe accurately to people who hadn't been in the room. Not surprise. Not solemnity. Something closer to recognition, the way you might look at someone you'd been writing letters to for years and were only now, finally, meeting in person.

"Happy birthday, Dr. Voss," he said. "Your actual one. Not the light-year version, though I understand the appeal." A faint, unmistakable warmth crossed whatever passed for his face. "Zero point oh-three light-years is a very small number to have built quite this much hope on top of. I thought that deserved acknowledging too."

Elara opened her mouth. Nothing especially useful came out.

"I'm Kael," he said, as if that settled anything at all. "I've been meaning to visit for some time. The timing matters more than the wanting to, in my experience, and the timing has only just now arrived."

"You're not—" She gestured, helplessly, at the space where his elbow should have solidly rested against the table and did not. "You're not actually here."

"No," he agreed, easily, without a trace of apology in it. "Not in the way you mean. But this is."

And then, without any visible motion at all — no outstretched hand, no gesture she could later reconstruct with any confidence — something appeared on the table in front of her. Small. Green-black. Glassy. Unlike anything she had ever held, and yet somehow, immediately, unmistakably familiar — the way a word can arrive in a language you've never spoken and still feel like it was always going to mean exactly this. It was solid enough that its slight weight dented the tablecloth beneath it.

The room did not breathe.

"I know," Kael said, and there was something in it that might, in a human throat, have been laughter.

Elara looked at the fragment. She looked at the shape of the man who had, by every available measure, just handed it to her without touching a single thing.

"Happy mili-light-year-travel-day, Dr. Voss," Kael said, and the warmth in it was entirely, uncomplicatedly real, whatever else about him wasn't.

Nobody in the mess hall said anything at all for a very long time.

Chapter Two — Crew and Craft

They moved the fragment, eventually, because standing around a mess hall table forever wasn't a viable long-term strategy, even for something like this. It ended up on a pedestal in the observation bay — backlit, top-lit, side-lit, every angle covered, the kind of lighting rig usually reserved for artifacts that had at least the decency to be housed behind museum glass. This one sat in the open air, unguarded, because nobody had yet come up with a container that made any practical sense for an object that didn't appear to want, need, or acknowledge containing.

The crew stood around it in a loose, unplanned circle. Nobody had called the gathering. Nobody had needed to.

The camera — if there had been a camera, and there was always a camera — would have caught a slow, unbroken pan across their faces: waiting, unreadable, faintly embarrassed by how long they'd been standing there in silence, as if the fragment might do something if they were patient enough, and equally afraid that it might do something if they weren't.

Seconds went by. Then minutes. Nobody moved.

"Have you touched it?" Mateo asked eventually, to nobody in particular and everybody at once. "Of course you touched it. You touched it in the mess hall, in front of everyone, thirty seconds after a projection handed it to you. What was it like?"

Elara didn't look away from the fragment. "It was like nothing."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the most accurate answer I have."

He made a small, exasperated sound that had a lot of history behind it, and some of that history went back further than either of them usually admitted out loud.

⁂

It had been a lab, once, a long time ago — a real one, with real hazard tape and a real supervising professor who had, against her own posted rules, let a promising but chronically underprepared second-year student stay late to finish an experiment that absolutely should have waited until morning. Elara had been that professor. She had also been, on that particular night, the person who reached for a sample she'd expressly told everyone else not to touch, because the readings had looked wrong in a way that made her curious instead of careful, which had always been her particular flaw, long before it became anyone else's problem.

It hadn't gone well. There had been a flash, a burn that took longer to heal than it should have, and a very frightened twenty-year-old Mateo Okoye — the same underprepared second-year, thrilled to be anywhere important at midnight — doing the only useful thing he knew how to do, which was hold her hand very tightly and apply exactly the wrong kind of bandage with tremendous confidence.

They had never really stopped being those two people, underneath everything else they'd since become.

Which was why, watching her stand in front of the fragment now, hands loose at her sides, doing the same thing she'd done that night — reaching for the thing everyone else knew better than to touch — Mateo felt something twist in his chest that had nothing to do with humor at all, even though humor was the only language he trusted himself to say it in.

"She won't let me touch it," said a small, put-upon voice from the vicinity of his wrist.

"Reggie."

"I'm just reporting the facts, Mateo. Accurately. As requested. Repeatedly."

Elara glanced over, one eyebrow raised in the specific way that meant she already knew exactly what was coming and was choosing to let it happen anyway. "You want to sample and laser it."

"I want to understand it," Mateo said, with the wounded dignity of a man whose actual plan had, in fact, involved a laser.

"No."

Mateo giggled — an actual giggle, undignified and immediate, the sound of a grown man who had just been caught red-handed and found the whole situation more delightful than embarrassing.

"I can't believe you still have that thing," Elara said, nodding at his wrist, at the small, scuffed housing that had clearly been through several generations of hardware without once being allowed to update its personality.

"Reggie's not a thing, Reggie's family."

"Reggie's a chatbot you built in your dorm room to help you finish problem sets, and you have kept upgrading the hardware underneath it for fifteen years rather than admit you're sentimental."

"Those are not mutually exclusive facts."

"I helped you build the first version."

"Which is exactly why I refuse to let it go. It's the only relationship I've maintained consistently since I was twenty-two, and I'm not examining that any further than I have to."

From the wrist, dryly: "I feel very seen right now, and also slightly hurt."

"See, this is what I put up with," Mateo said to the room, to the fragment, to no one.

⁂

The pan resumed — the long, slow drift of attention back to the object on the pedestal, back to the silence that had settled over the room before Mateo had broken it and would clearly settle again now that he'd finished. Nobody had moved any closer to the fragment. Nobody had moved any further away, either. It simply sat there, doing the thing it apparently did best, which was absolutely nothing, in a way that felt, somehow, like it was doing quite a lot.

"Camping detected," said a second voice, cooler and more even than Reggie's, coming from the room's general address system rather than anyone's wrist. "Please resume productive behavior."

They laughed, mostly out of relief that laughing was still an option.

"Eliza, we've been standing here for four minutes," Mateo said.

"Four minutes and forty-one seconds. I've flagged it as recreational loitering. Would you like me to schedule a follow-up reminder in another four minutes and forty-one seconds?"

"Please don't."

"Acknowledged. Reminder cancelled."

Elara allowed herself, finally, something close to a smile — small, tired, real. "Why did I let you program both models?"

"You didn't let me do anything," Mateo said. "You were fully capable of building Eliza from scratch yourself, and you know it. You just requested a few tweaks and let me take the credit, which, frankly, is the most generous thing anyone's ever done for my ego."

"I wanted her to sound a little less like you."

"She sounds exactly like me."

"I said a little less."

Eliza, unbothered, added: "For the record, I consider myself my own person. Reggie's just my rougher draft."

"I heard that," said Reggie.

"You were meant to."

The fragment sat where it had sat the entire time, backlit, top-lit, side-lit, saying nothing at all, while around it the only two artificial minds on the ship that anyone had bothered to give a personality to settled into what was, by every available metric, an argument older than either of them technically was.

Elara looked at it a moment longer. Whatever she'd expected coming into this room — answers, explanations, some flicker of understanding she could carry back to a report — none of it had arrived, and somehow, standing there in the quiet argument of borrowed voices and inherited jokes, she found she didn't mind nearly as much as she probably should have.

The ship carried on, the way ships did, indifferent to the small, strange thing sitting patiently in its brightest room, waiting — for what, exactly, none of them yet knew enough to ask.

Chapter Three — What Am I Missing

It took less than a week for the fragment to stop being an event and start being furniture.

That was, Elara privately thought, one of the stranger things about people — not that they could get used to almost anything, which she'd always known, but how quickly the getting-used-to happened when the alternative was standing around forever in a state of low-grade awe. The pedestal in the observation bay eventually lost its dedicated lighting rig to a maintenance request that had nothing to do with the fragment and everything to do with somebody needing the fixtures for an actual repair job. Nobody objected. The fragment didn't seem to mind being demoted to ordinary light. She wasn't sure it minded anything.

She'd started keeping it with her, on the nights she couldn't quite make herself leave it alone in an empty room. It sat now at the center of the Vigil, alongside the badge rather than instead of it — two small green things that had no business belonging to the same family and somehow, unmistakably, did.

The Horizon Echo did what it had always done, which was keep moving.

⁂

"Eleventh candidate," Mateo announced, dropping into the seat beside her with the particular cheerfulness of a man who had genuinely never lost his appetite for this part of the job, whatever else the last week had done to everyone else's. "Out of fifty-seven flagged systems. We are, if anyone's counting, exactly nineteen point three percent of the way through humanity's most expensive scavenger hunt."

"Somebody's counting."

"Somebody is always counting. It's in my job description, right under emotional support and above technically also does maintenance."

Eliza's voice, evenly, from the panel: "For the record, the detection array Mateo is referring to was designed primarily by Dr. Voss's own family line, three generations back. I only mention this because I suspect it will come up again later and someone should have said it first."

"Thank you, Eliza."

"You're welcome. I try to be useful."

Elara pulled the summary up on the main display, the way she had ten times before and would, presumably, another forty-six times before this particular hunt was finished. The numbers laid themselves out the way numbers always did — clean, indifferent, entirely unbothered by what they actually meant.

One world, unchanged. Same biosignature as their last pass, same quiet, patient hum of life going about its business without any indication it had noticed being watched.

Three worlds carrying what the array flagged as green noise — a faint, inconclusive shimmer at the edge of detectable, nothing like a full Shift, more like static that hadn't decided yet whether it wanted to become a signal. Baseline intelligent life, still very much present, still very much itself.

And seven. Seven worlds that had already done what Epsilon-9-4 had done in front of them — veiled, quieted, slipped below the threshold that made a planet worth a second look. Seven, out of eleven, in a survey that had only just gotten started.

"That's not a good ratio," Mateo said, quietly enough that it landed differently than his usual commentary.

"No," Elara agreed. "It isn't."

⁂

They caught the twelfth mid-transit, three days later, between one gravity assist and the next — Elara half-watching a status feed she wasn't required to be watching, the way you keep half an eye on a kettle you already know is going to boil.

"Twelfth candidate initiating," Eliza reported, and there was no cheer in it this time, not because Eliza had ever had any to begin with, but because the room itself seemed to have quietly agreed that cheer wasn't the correct register anymore.

Mateo didn't make a joke. That, more than the readout itself, was what told Elara how the room actually felt about it.

The thirteenth came four days after that, faster than the pattern should have technically allowed if the pattern were staying consistent, which was itself the most unsettling part. Not just more worlds Shifting. Faster. The gap between candidates closing in a way that made the whole survey feel less like careful, patient observation and more like watching something accelerate toward a conclusion nobody aboard had been told the shape of yet.

"Thirteen," Mateo said, staring at the display like it had personally done something to him. "That's not a trend anymore. That's a slope."

"It's always been a slope," Elara said. "We just weren't standing close enough to see the angle."

"That's not comforting."

"I didn't say it to comfort you."

⁂

She found herself back at the Vigil that night, later than usual, later than she'd meant to be. Science to her left — the slide rule, the crystal, the vial labeled in handwriting five generations old. Family to her right — the photographs, curling at their corners despite every promise the climate control system made about humidity control. And at the center, badge and fragment together, doing what they always did, which was nothing at all, and somehow managing to fill the room with it anyway.

She didn't reach for either one. She just looked — past the science, past the strange, patient object that had upended a week of her life without lifting a finger to do it, and landed, the way she always eventually did, on the photographs. Faces she'd never met. A woman with her grandmother's exact jaw, standing in front of an early prototype solar array that looked, by modern standards, almost embarrassingly primitive. A man beside her, unnamed on the back of the photo, smiling like he already knew something the camera couldn't quite capture.

Thirteen worlds. Seven gone quiet. The gap closing faster than it should.

"What am I missing?" she asked the photographs, quietly, the way you ask a question you already suspect has an answer you haven't earned yet.

Nobody answered. The badge didn't warm. The fragment didn't glow. The ship hummed on around her, indifferent and patient, carrying her steadily toward whatever came next.

Chapter Four — The Believers of the Voss Line

Esther Voss was nineteen years old, badly under-slept, and holding a sample she had been told, explicitly and in writing, not to touch.

The lab was the kind that only ever existed at universities too proud of their own legacy to properly fund the safety upgrades everyone kept requesting — good bones, bad wiring, a hazard-tape dispenser that had clearly seen more use than the fire extinguisher beside it. It was well past midnight. It was well past the hour any reasonable second-year student should have still been on campus. Esther was not, by her own later admission, a reasonable second-year student.

The reading on the panel in front of her didn't make sense. That was the whole problem — not that it was dangerous, exactly, but that it was wrong in a way her mind refused to leave alone. Solar collection efficiency that spiked, inexplicably, in wavelengths the equipment shouldn't have been sensitive to at all. A voice inside her, the same voice that would apparently get passed down the family line like a bad debt, said: just a little closer.

The flash, when it came, wasn't large. It didn't need to be. It caught her across the forearm, burned deeper than it should have for something so brief, and left her sitting on the lab floor with her ears ringing and her vision swimming green at the edges in a way that took considerably longer to fade than it should have.

Professor Kevin Richards found her there eleven minutes later, having been the only other person foolish enough to still be in the building.

He didn't yell. That was the detail Esther would repeat to her own daughter decades later, and her daughter would repeat to hers, until it had worn as smooth and certain as a river stone: he didn't yell, he just knelt down, and he looked at me like he already knew exactly what I'd been reaching for.

"You weren't supposed to touch that," he said, wrapping the burn in gauze with hands steadier than the situation warranted.

"I know."

"You're going to do it again anyway, aren't you."

It wasn't really a question. Esther, cradling her arm, found she didn't have the energy left to lie to him.

"Good," Richards said, in a tone she never fully explained afterward, not even to her own family, not even at the very end. "Someone has to."

⁂

What Esther built, in the years that followed — slowly, stubbornly, funded by grants nobody quite understood the purpose of and personal savings nobody quite approved of — was a transmitter. Or a receiver. Or, more accurately, something that hadn't quite decided which it wanted to be yet, a panel that drank in more of the light spectrum than anything else on the market and gave back more than it should have in return. First successful yield, she wrote on the label of the very first sample that worked, in handwriting her great-great-granddaughter would one day trace with her own fingertip in a quiet room aboard a ship Esther could never have imagined.

Her daughter, Linda, took the panels apart and put them back together with something new folded in — a way of drawing on frequencies further out along the spectrum than visible light had ever bothered to reach, EMS absorption where there had only ever been sunlight before. Linda never touched anything she'd been told not to. She simply widened what there was to touch.

Erika, Linda's daughter, solved a problem nobody had asked her to solve: how to take that absorbed energy and coax a little water out of thin, uncooperative air, a trick that made her briefly, locally famous in drought seasons nobody else's tech could touch. She kept a framed photo of Esther's original burn scar — an actual photograph of the scar itself, faded and a little morbid — on her office wall, captioned only: the reason we started.

Lindsay, Erika's daughter, added the piece that turned water into something closer to sustenance — glucose, simple sugars, the barest beginning of an idea nobody in the family had said out loud yet, though every one of them had quietly wondered it: what if a person didn't have to eat, either. Lindsay was the one who first pinned Esther's original badge — reforged, generation over generation, always the same leaf-shape, always the same faint iridescent shimmer — to her own collar every morning, the way you'd carry a question you hadn't finished asking.

And then there was Elara.

She hadn't set out to perfect anything. She'd only meant to make the receivers a little more sensitive, a little more precise, the way every generation before her had nudged the family's strange inheritance one careful degree further than the last. It was almost an accident, the night she realized the panels weren't just gathering ambient light and stray radio noise anymore.

They were catching something else. Something faint, and patterned, and very, very far away.

She sat with that reading for a long time before she told anyone.

Chapter Five — Faint Signals

Kael arrived at Elara's eleventh listed candidate the way he arrived everywhere — without transition, without the small mercy of a journey in between, simply here after having just been elsewhere. He didn't bother learning what anyone called it. There was no reason to spend the attention on a name for a place he was only passing through, however briefly he might come to love it in the hours he had left to spend there. His own instruments didn't bother with atmosphere or oceans or the particular chemistry of a world's air, either. They asked a different question entirely.

"Emergent signature: significant, and rising," his ship reported, flat and unhurried, entirely uninterested in whether the news was good. "Coherence gradient across the primary population cluster accelerating past committed threshold. Resource-consumption curve inverted — want falling faster than supply, first time this cycle. Local transmission: single source, rhythmic, degrading toward silence. Projected time to full coherence: hours."

Hours. Kael settled into the quiet the number implied, unhurried himself, watching the small blue-green world turn beneath him with the specific fondness of someone revisiting a place he'd never technically been to before and somehow already loved.

It was quieter than most. He'd learned, over more of these than he now bothered counting, that some worlds went out singing — a whole civilization's noise rising together right up until the moment it simply wasn't noise anymore. This one was going quiet instead, the way a room empties when everyone in it has already privately decided to leave and is simply waiting, politely, for the others to notice.

The rhythmic pulse — the single, degrading source his ship kept flagging — was the last of it. Some ancient piece of long-range sonar, still faithfully pinging into the dark long after anyone below had stopped needing it to.

⁂

Three days later — a span of time that meant considerably more to the crew of the Horizon Echo than it did to the being currently drifting toward their eleventh candidate from a completely different angle of approach — Elara sat with a cooling cup of something technically classified as coffee, watching the same planet do the same impossible, quiet thing.

She didn't know, of course, that some tens of thousands of kilometers away and immeasurably outside her instruments' reach, someone else was watching it too. She only knew that it felt, for reasons she couldn't articulate to Mateo without sounding faintly unhinged, like being watched back.

She raised her cup, once, to no one in particular. It was a habit she'd never examined too closely.

Kael, at more or less the same moment, allowed himself something that might have been a sip of nothing at all — a gesture with no beverage attached to it, kept purely because some habits outlast the need for them.

Neither of them noticed the coincidence. Neither of them were meant to. Not yet.

⁂

The twelfth candidate came faster than protocol on either side quite expected.

Kael's ship logged it with the same flat efficiency as the first: "Emergent signature: significant. Coherence gradient: already past committed threshold on arrival. Local transmission source: absent — this cluster made considerably less noise than most, historically." He found that detail mildly interesting, in the specific way he found most things mildly interesting these days, and drifted closer anyway, unhurried, watching the pattern accelerate without alarm.

Aboard the Horizon Echo, Mateo drummed his fingers against the console in the exact rhythm — though he couldn't have known it — that Kael was, at that same moment, tapping against nothing at all, keeping time with a song only one of them could hear.

"Twelfth candidate initiating," Eliza reported, cheerless in a way that had become the room's new normal.

Mateo didn't make a joke. Kael, watching the same event from an angle no instrument aboard the Echo could have plotted, found himself faintly amused by the shape of the silence anyway — the specific, universal quality of two crews, on opposite ends of understanding, running out of things to say at exactly the same moment.

⁂

The thirteenth was where the noise started.

Kael's ship flagged it before he'd even fully arrived: "Emergent signature: significant, accelerating. Additional transmission detected — non-native, artificial, directional. Source: third planet, inner system, currently under separate observation." A pause, and then, with the same flat indifference it applied to everything: "Multiple additional transmissions detected. Same source. Increasing in frequency."

Kael understood immediately what he was listening to, in the way you understand a language you learned so long ago you've forgotten there was ever a time you didn't know it. Somewhere, on a small, loud, blue planet, someone had detected something — one of these faint dying signals, the exact kind he'd just watched fade to nothing twice already — and had done the thing that species like this one always eventually did.

They'd shouted back.

Not just once. Not just toward Epsilon-9-4's own fading sonar ping, but toward every faint, patterned thing their improving instruments had lately started to notice, aimed out into the dark with all the hopeful, indiscriminate volume of a species that had never quite learned the difference between listening and waiting to be heard.

"I'm going to need to do something about that," Kael murmured, mostly to himself, mostly fond despite the mild, genuine annoyance underneath it. "Politely. Eventually."

⁂

Aboard the Horizon Echo, Elara stared at the readout with an expression Mateo had learned, over the years, meant she was doing complicated math she didn't yet trust enough to say out loud.

"Thirteen," Mateo said again, quieter this time. "That's not a trend. That's a slope."

She didn't answer right away. Somewhere in the back of her mind, unbidden, a thought arrived that she couldn't trace to any actual evidence: something is answering, out there. Or something is about to.

She had no way of knowing how close to correct that was, or how little it had to do with anything her instruments could measure.

⁂

Kael's ship spoke again, some while later, without any particular ceremony attached to the words at all, the way it delivered every piece of information it had ever given him, urgent or otherwise.

"Recalculating sequence position, incorporating latest transmission source." A brief pause — not hesitation, nothing so human as that, simply the shape processing takes when there's genuinely nothing more to add. "Designation: G2V-45-C. Sequence position: fourteenth."

Kael looked, for a long moment, at the small blue-green world doing all that shouting.

"Fourteenth," he repeated. "G2V-45-C." Neither word came out flat.

The designation sat oddly in his mouth, the way any name does before you've earned the right version of it. He didn't yet know what they called it themselves. He suspected, given everything he'd just listened to, that he was about to find out whether they'd like him asking.

⁂

Chapter Six — What the Cats See

At 0600 local — a time that meant nothing at all in open transit and everything the moment a cat decided it should — something heavy landed directly on Elara's sternum and announced, with tremendous personal conviction, that breakfast was now a matter of some urgency.

"Kita," she said, without opening her eyes, "he can feed you too, you know."

Kita did not appear to find this argument persuasive. He never did. He simply sat there, a small, dense weight of pure expectation, purring with the specific patience of an animal who had never once in his life needed to raise his voice to get what he wanted.

She'd stopped, months ago, trying to explain to anyone outside the ship what it had taken to actually name him. Mateo had gone through an entire certification review — a real one, with a real committee, mildly baffled by the sheer administrative effort a junior engineer was willing to expend over a stray his sister's cousin's neighbor had found half-starved near a shipping dock — before landing, triumphantly, on Taumatawhakatangihangakoauauotamateaturipukakapikimaungahoronukupokaiwhenuakitanatahu. Kita, for short. A hill in New Zealand, not a star, which had disappointed absolutely nobody except the one crew member who'd been hoping for something more astronomically appropriate.

"You wanted the longest name in the cosmos," she'd told him at the time, "and you found the longest name on Earth instead."

"I never said cosmos specifically," Mateo had replied, entirely unbothered. "I said longest. I stand by the research."

Elara scratched behind one dark ear, and Kita's purring deepened into something that vibrated all the way through the mattress.

"Fine," she muttered. "Breakfast."

⁂

Mateo was already awake when she reached the galley, which was rare enough on its own to be worth remarking on, hunched over a display with the particular focus of a man doing math he was mildly annoyed by.

"We've got a close approach coming up," he said, not looking away from the screen. "Earth. Gravity assist, standard trajectory, nothing exciting. We'll skim past close enough for the old sentimental instruments to pick up a decent signal home if anyone wants to send actual letters instead of compressed data packets, which, statistically, nobody will."

"I might."

"You say that every time."

"I mean it every time."

"That's what makes it sad, Elara." He finally looked up, offering her the particular half-grin that meant he was about to say something he found funnier than she would. "Anyway. We'll be close enough to smell the place, cosmically speaking, and then it's back out for candidate fourteen — sorry, fifteen, sixteen, whatever number we're actually on by the time we get there. I've genuinely lost count and I built the counter."

"Fifty-seven total. We're not even a fifth of the way through."

"See, this is why I don't ask you the numbers. You always actually know them."

⁂

She fed Kita — real food, not the flour-adjacent cake situation from a few days prior, which he had shown zero interest in regardless — and carried her tea back toward her quarters, toward the Vigil, toward the quiet hour she still allowed herself most mornings even when the rest of the day had stopped cooperating.

The fragment sat where it always sat now, badge beside it, both of them doing what they did best, which was absolutely nothing that any instrument aboard had ever managed to measure.

Kita followed her in, unhurried, and settled himself in the narrow strip of floor directly beside the Vigil's stand — a spot he'd claimed for himself weeks ago and defended, on the rare occasions anyone tried to move him, with the quiet, immovable confidence of an animal who understood something about the arrangement that nobody had bothered explaining to him.

She sat. She let her tea cool, the way she always let it cool, distracted by nothing in particular and everything at once.

Kita's purring, this close to the fragment, took on a quality she'd noticed before and never quite mentioned out loud to anyone — a low, steady thrum that seemed to sit at almost exactly the same frequency as whatever the fragment did when it caught the light just right. She'd checked, once, half-embarrassed at herself for checking. The numbers were close enough that she'd stopped trying to explain the difference away as coincidence and simply stopped looking for an explanation at all.

His fur, in the dim morning light, was the exact color of the space outside the viewport — not merely black, but deep in a way that seemed to actually recede, the way looking at him too long felt oddly like looking at something with real distance behind it rather than simple darkness.

He turned his head. Their eyes met.

For one single, unmistakable instant, his eyes flared — a bright, unmistakable flash of green, there and gone almost before she could be certain she'd seen it — before settling back into a low, subtle glow that lingered a beat longer than she was entirely comfortable with.

"What do you see in there, kiddo?" she asked, quietly, the way you ask a question of someone you already suspect won't answer, but might, one day, if you kept asking gently enough.

Kita blinked, slow and deliberate, the particular blink she'd read about once, years ago, in some half-remembered corner of the ship's old data archives — the one that meant trust, the one that meant I am not afraid of you, and I would like you to know it.

He didn't answer, of course. He never did. He simply settled his head back down against her knee, purring on at that same quiet, familiar frequency, and let her keep not-knowing in peace, the way he always had, for as long as she'd known him.

Outside the viewport, Earth turned slowly closer, small and blue and entirely unaware of everything that was about to be asked of it.

⁂

Chapter Seven — Arrival, Unnoticed

Some while after the thirteenth shift's numbers had finally stopped moving — long enough that the crew had allowed themselves to believe, cautiously, that they'd been granted a genuine lull — the Horizon Echo settled into the particular rhythm of a ship with nothing especially urgent left to do. Maintenance logs got caught up. A recurring rattle in one of the aft panels finally got tracked down and silenced. Mateo spent an entire, deeply satisfying afternoon recalibrating a sensor array that had needed it for months, humming something tuneless and content the whole time.

It lasted four days.

The alarm, when it came, wasn't loud so much as it was insistent — a low, patient tone that didn't so much interrupt the ship's quiet as simply refuse to stop until someone acknowledged it.

"Multiple readings, unclassified," Eliza reported, and there was something almost apologetic in the way she said it, as though she understood, in whatever way she was capable of understanding anything, that unclassified was not the answer anyone wanted from her. "Signature bears partial resemblance to prior anomalous readings logged against the badge and fragment materials. Confidence: low. I want to be clear that I am guessing, and that I do not enjoy guessing."

"You've never guessed in your life," Mateo said, already moving toward the bridge at a pace just short of running. "This is exciting for you. Don't pretend otherwise."

"I contain multitudes."

Elara and Mateo nearly collided entering the bridge, drawn by the same unspecific, prickling certainty that something was about to happen, without any of them being able to point to the exact reading that had convinced them of it.

"Anyone have theories?" Mateo asked the room at large.

"No," said Reggie, from his wrist, with an air of tremendous patience. "Please. You guys first. I insist. I've been dying to hear what you've all got, and I promise not to laugh at any of it more than strictly necessary."

Nobody answered him. Nobody had anything to answer with.

And then, without any warning at all — no flicker, no static, none of the half-second lag that had marked every one of Kael's projections since the mess hall — a man was simply standing at the front of the bridge, solid, unmistakably present, casting an actual shadow across the deck plating that hadn't been there a heartbeat before.

The difference registered on every face in the room at once, though it took a moment for any of them to articulate exactly what the difference was. This wasn't a green-tinged suggestion of a person, translucent at the edges, half in the room and half somewhere else entirely. This was weight. This was a shadow that fell the way shadows were supposed to fall.

"Hello," Kael said, and this time his voice arrived precisely when his mouth moved, no lag at all, no gap between the sound and the source of it.

Mateo recovered first, because Mateo always did, one way or another.

"Welcome to Earth," he said, and something in the plainness of it — no ceremony, no grand speech, just the simplest possible thing one person could offer another arriving somewhere new — seemed to catch Kael slightly off guard, in a way none of the sensor readings or dramatic entrances had managed.

"Earth," Kael repeated, testing the shape of it. Something crossed his face that might have been delight, might have been relief, might have been both at once. "Thank you. I've been calling it something considerably less charming."

He turned, then, and found Elara with the same unhurried certainty he'd found her the first time, in the mess hall, weeks and a lifetime ago.

"Dr. Voss," he said. "It is an honor and a pleasure to finally meet you properly. I am Kael Ren." A pause, brief and deliberate. "May I see your Vigil?"

⁂

They walked together, the two of them, down corridors that suddenly felt smaller than Elara remembered them being, though nothing about the ship had changed at all. She was aware, distantly, of Mateo trailing a respectful few steps behind, of Reggie's small light blinking steadily at his wrist in a way that suggested he was recording. Documentation seemed like the last thing on anyone's mind.

The badge began glowing before they'd even reached her door.

It wasn't the soft, familiar shimmer she'd grown used to over years of quiet mornings. This was something closer to a pulse — brightening and dimming in a slow, deliberate rhythm, like something breathing for the first time in a very long while.

Kael stopped in the doorway and simply looked at it for a long moment, an expression on his face she would spend a very long time afterward trying to name and never quite managing. Not surprise. Something closer to recognition, arriving at last, exactly where it had always been headed.

"May I?" he asked, and lowered himself, without waiting for an answer he seemed to already trust, into the exact cross-legged posture she'd held every morning of her adult life. He gestured to the space across from him.

"Please. Join me."

She sat.

⁂

What followed was not, in any strict sense, a conversation. It moved the way real conversations rarely did — circling back on itself, doubling into silences that didn't feel empty so much as full, full of things neither of them had quite found language for yet.

She told him about Esther, eventually, because it seemed impossible not to, sitting where she was sitting, across from what she was sitting across from. The lab. The burn. Professor Richards, and the way he'd knelt down instead of yelling.

Kael listened to all of it with an attention that felt, somehow, entirely unhurried, as though he had nowhere else to be and never had.

"She built something remarkable," he said, when she'd finished. "All of them did. Each generation, adding one true thing to what came before."

"You knew," Elara said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I knew a great many things arrived faster than they might have," Kael said, and there was something careful in the way he chose the words, something that stopped just short of a door she sensed he had no intention of opening for her, not yet, maybe not ever. "I've watched this happen slowly, on other worlds. Painfully slowly, some of them. Centuries where it might have taken decades. Decades where it might have taken years." He paused, and the pleasure in his voice, when it came, was unmistakable and entirely unguarded. "Your family did this in five generations. I confess I did not expect that. I am — considerably pleased by it, if I'm honest with you."

"Pleased how?"

"The way you're pleased," he said, simply, "when something you've hoped for arrives sooner than you dared to plan around."

She wanted to press further. She found, sitting across from him, that she didn't quite have it in her to make it an interrogation. Some questions, she was beginning to understand, weren't going to be answered by asking them more insistently. They were only going to be answered, if they ever were, by however much time it took to earn the answer honestly.

Behind them, unnoticed by either, Reggie's small light continued its patient, steady blink, recording every word of it — the grief, the badge's slow, breathing pulse, Kael's careful, unguarded pleasure — onto a drive that would, in time, belong to considerably more people than the two of them currently sitting in that quiet, overfull room.

⁂

Chapter Eight — The Fragment

"So," Elara said, once the silence had gone on long enough that someone needed to be the one to break it, "are you human?"

"I was," Kael said. "And still can be, when I want to. As you observe me here and now, I am different. I am everywhere, all the time, to me. It doesn't always translate well into a single tense."

Mateo, who had been hovering in the doorway trying very hard to look like a man who wasn't hanging on every word, lost the battle entirely.

"Interactive Universe Sandbox," he said, delighted. "Sick."

"I don't know what that means," Kael said, "but I appreciate the enthusiasm behind it."

"It means you're basically running your own reality on god-mode and I respect it enormously."

They compared notes, after that, in a way that felt almost domestic — the eleventh candidate, the twelfth, the thirteenth, each of them describing what they'd seen from their own side of an event neither had known the other was watching. Kael's account came dressed in language none of them fully understood — coherence gradients, resource-consumption curves inverting — and Elara's came dressed in the language of pressure readings and biosignature curves, and somewhere in the overlap of the two, both of them found themselves, unexpectedly, laughing at the same details for entirely different reasons.

"So what is this," Elara asked eventually, reaching out — the same old reflex, the same old flaw, reaching for the thing she probably shouldn't — and let her fingers pass through the space where his arm should have solidly been.

Kael didn't flinch away from the question. He answered it the way he seemed to answer everything, with total, unbothered authority.

"It is everything," he said. "Potential. Energy. Light. Shade. Whatever you want most, as long as it leads to the increased probability of emergent property success."

Nobody in the room had a follow-up question ready. It took a moment — several moments — before anyone managed to say anything at all, and even then, what came out wasn't really a question so much as a small, collective exhale.

⁂

His time ran out the way it always seemed to, without any visible countdown, simply arriving at its edge the way a tide arrives at a shoreline whether anyone's watching or not.

He paused, and glanced, almost as an afterthought, toward the fragment still sitting quietly on its pedestal.

"Any luck figuring that out yet?" he asked, and there was real, warm amusement in it, nothing unkind at all.

What he didn't say — what none of them would ever hear him say, not that day and not any day after — was how thoroughly he'd expected exactly this. He had watched enough of these unfoldings, across enough worlds, to know precisely what a species did with something they didn't understand and couldn't break. They took it apart. They lasered it, dissolved it, bombarded it with everything they had, the way a child takes apart a new toy not out of malice but out of the same fierce, ordinary curiosity that had built every good thing this particular species had ever managed. He had left it for them anyway. He had left it because of that, not in spite of it — a gift given in full knowledge that it would be handled roughly, tested destructively, poked and prodded and demanded of, long before anyone thought to simply sit with it and ask it nicely what it wanted to become. That was, in its own way, exactly the point. You didn't hand a child a toy and expect reverence. You handed them a toy and waited, patiently, for the version of them that eventually stopped needing to break things in order to understand them.

"Not yet," Elara said, entirely unaware of any of that.

"You will," Kael said, and it didn't sound like a prediction so much as a simple statement of fact he'd already watched happen enough times to trust completely.

They walked him back to the bridge, and for the first time, his ship came fully into view — not the half-glimpsed silhouette from Chapter One's projection, not a shadow implied by sensors that refused to commit to a shape, but the whole of it, obsidian and seamless, catching a faint green-blue shimmer across its surface that matched, unmistakably, the exact quality of light that moved across Kael himself.

He reached out, then, and touched the surface of his own ship — a small, unhurried gesture, palm flat against obsidian — and in the other room, on its pedestal, the fragment answered, a faint ripple of the same green-blue light passing across its surface in perfect, silent unison.

Nobody had a word for that either.

The ship began, slowly, to fold itself back into the space it had come from — not moving so much as simply agreeing to be somewhere else — and Kael went with it, the way he'd arrived: without ceremony, without a countdown, simply here and then, gently, not.

⁂

The bridge sat in silence for a long moment after he'd gone.

"Reggie," Elara said finally, not looking away from the space where the ship had been, "did you get all that?"

Mateo's eyes went comically wide. For one full second, he looked like a man caught somewhere between mortified and thrilled, mouth working around the beginning of several different sentences before he settled on none of them, visibly fighting down a laugh that wanted very badly to become something louder and far less dignified.

"I—" he managed. "Yes. Obviously. I was always going to. I just wasn't going to bring it up, because bringing it up felt—"

"You should have brought it up."

"In my defense, there was a lot going on."

"Reggie."

"Full 4K," Reggie confirmed, entirely unbothered by the entire exchange. "Audio's clean. I even caught the ripple effect on the fragment, if anyone's interested. Would anyone like a highlight reel?"

"No," Elara and Mateo said, at exactly the same moment, in exactly the same tone, and Mateo finally let the laugh out, helpless and relieved, the sound of it filling the quiet the ship's departure had left behind.

Somewhere considerably further away than either of them had any reason to imagine, a different recording — of a different kind, on a different scale entirely — was still years from being made. But it would be made. And when it was, someone else, in a very different room, would ask almost exactly the same question, and get an answer that changed considerably more than a bridge full of people trying not to laugh.

⁂

Chapter Nine — Aftermath

Ross Murphy's basement had not, strictly speaking, been a basement in a long time. It had become, over the better part of a decade, something closer to a shrine — a low-ceilinged room lined wall to wall with antenna diagrams, hand-labeled frequency logs, and a receiver rig that had been rebuilt so many times it no longer resembled the modest kit he'd started with. He shared the space, most nights, with two men who had long since stopped needing an invitation to show up — Dale, who trusted nothing he hadn't personally soldered, and Priya's husband Tomás, who'd wandered in seven years ago looking for a missing cable and simply never fully left.

"HANNAH," Ross said, not looking up from the console, "run the eleventh again. I want the raw waveform, not the cleaned version."

"You always want the raw waveform," said HANNAH, the rig's own modest AI, her voice carrying the patient, faintly amused tone of someone who had said this exact sentence more times than she'd bothered counting. "The cleaned version is cleaned for a reason, Ross."

"The cleaned version is somebody else's opinion of what matters."

"That somebody else is generally correct."

"Generally isn't always."

Dale, from the corner, didn't look up from his soldering either. "She's not wrong, though."

"Nobody asked you, Dale."

They'd caught the eleventh candidate three weeks ago now — faint, garbled, a signature that took real, patient work to separate from the noise floor, but unmistakably there once you knew what you were listening for. The twelfth had come easier, cleaner, close enough to the same frequency band that HANNAH had flagged it automatically before any of them had touched the dial. The thirteenth had arrived faster than either of the other two, and louder, and Ross had sat with his headphones on for the better part of an hour afterward, not saying anything at all, the way he did when something had actually gotten to him rather than merely interested him.

"That's three," Tomás said quietly, setting down the cable he'd been working on. "In under two weeks."

"I know."

"Nobody official's saying anything."

"I know that too."

HANNAH's voice, gentler than usual: "For what it's worth, Ross, the pattern you flagged eight years ago is holding. I ran the comparison again last night. You were right."

Ross didn't answer that one at all. He'd spent the better part of a decade being told, gently or otherwise, that he wasn't. Being right, it turned out, didn't feel the way he'd always imagined it would.

⁂

Houston received the same three signatures through channels considerably more official than a rebuilt basement rig, and treated them, for the most part, as exactly what the paperwork said they were: data. Olivia Higgins logged all three into the daily summary without particular ceremony, the way she logged everything — thoroughly, accurately, and without any interest in editorializing about what any of it might eventually mean.

"Three confirmed shifts, candidates eleven through thirteen," she noted, forwarding the summary onward through the usual distribution list. "Acceleration noted, flagged for review at next scheduling window."

Nobody at the next scheduling window would have much to say about it either, not yet. That, too, was simply how these things tended to go, until suddenly they didn't.

⁂

Elara was in the lab when the recall came through — sleeves pushed up, half a dozen readings open across the display, deep enough into a new line of testing on the fragment that she didn't immediately register the sound of the door.

"Elara." Mateo, from the doorway, doing a poor job of hiding whatever expression was underneath the one he'd chosen to lead with. "They—" He cleared his throat, visibly reconsidering a word already halfway out of his mouth. "Request our immediate counsel on-planet. For debrief."

She looked up. "You were going to say demand."

"I was absolutely going to say demand."

"Which one was it actually?"

"Officially? Request. The word on the actual transmission was request." He leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, the picture of a man trying very hard to sound unbothered. "The tone underneath the word, however, suggested that request was doing a considerable amount of diplomatic labor it did not actually earn."

Elara set down the instrument she'd been holding and looked, for a long moment, at the fragment sitting quietly on its bench, entirely indifferent to the fact that it had just become considerably more complicated to keep studying in peace.

"How long do we have?"

"Long enough to pack. Not long enough to argue."

⁂

She stopped at the church on her way through, before the shuttle, because it felt, in a way she couldn't have fully explained to Mateo without a much longer conversation than either of them had time for, like the kind of thing you did before walking into a room full of people who wanted something from you they didn't yet know how to ask for cleanly.

The building looked the same as it always had — same worn pews, same stained light falling in the same familiar shapes across the same old floor. But there was a tension in the room now that hadn't been there the last time she'd visited, low and unspoken, the particular quiet of people who had a great many questions and had not yet agreed among themselves which ones were safe to ask out loud.

She didn't stay long. She didn't need to, to feel it.

Whatever was coming, it had already arrived here first, in its own quiet way, long before the shuttle ever left the ground.

⁂

Chapter Ten — Studying the Glimmer

The fragment traveled in a shoebox. Kita's shoebox — that he seemed to approve of, having inspected the whole operation from a nearby shelf with an air of tremendous supervisory authority.

It was, at least, the right size, and the only cardboard box readily available anywhere on the ship, on account of the fact that Kita's box got refreshed with near-religious regularity every time anyone made a supply run and came home with something new to strip for parts. This one still smelled faintly of packing tape. Mateo tucked a jacket underneath it for padding, insisting, with total sincerity, that this counted as appropriate cushioning protocol.

"It's the single most significant object currently known to exist," Elara said, watching him tuck the flaps closed.

"It's also apparently indestructible. I checked the logs. Nobody's managed to so much as scratch it. I think the shoebox is fine."

"That is not the point, Mateo."

"I know it's not the point. I'm simply observing that the point and the shoebox are, in this specific instance, not mutually exclusive."

⁂

The debrief room, when they reached it, was full of the specific kind of person Elara had learned, over a handful of very long years, to recognize on sight — the sort who wore urgency the way other people wore cologne, applied deliberately, in careful amounts, meant to be noticed before they'd said a single word. A Deputy Director of something. A liaison from something else. Three people whose actual titles she never fully caught and suspected, privately, didn't matter nearly as much as the fact that they'd been sent.

The meeting had not yet properly started when someone noticed the shoebox sitting, unassumingly, on the table between them.

"Is that," said the Deputy Director, in the tone of a man carefully constructing a sentence he did not want to finish, "it?"

"It travels well," Mateo offered.

What followed was less a debrief than a controlled outbreak of institutional horror — the specific, escalating panic of several very important people simultaneously realizing that an object of genuinely unknown properties had been transported through open atmosphere, past open air, into a building full of other human beings, in a cardboard box, with a jacket in it.

"Quarantine protocol," someone was saying, several times, with increasing volume. "There is no record of quarantine protocol being followed. There is no record of any protocol being followed."

"In our defense," Mateo said, "there wasn't a protocol yet to follow. We were sort of hoping you'd have one."

Nobody found that especially funny except Mateo.

The directive that emerged from the wreckage of the meeting was simple, urgent, and delivered with the particular fervor of people trying very hard to look like they'd been in control of the situation the entire time: the fragment was to be studied, immediately, under whatever rushed containment they could assemble in the next several hours, and then returned — swiftly, quietly, with considerably more ceremony than it had arrived with — to the Horizon Echo, where it apparently belonged in everyone's estimation except the estimation of the people who'd actually met it.

⁂

Three teams ended up working in adjacent rooms, in the particular controlled chaos of people who knew they had hours, not weeks, and intended to make every one of them count.

The materials team went first, and got nothing. Mass spectrometry returned a baseline indistinguishable from the room's own ambient air, as though the instrument had simply declined to notice anything sitting in front of it at all. X-ray diffraction found no lattice, no structure, nothing to map — the scan behaving as if pointed at empty space occupied, somehow, by a very convincing shadow. Thermal imaging returned flat, unremarkable readings, and one technician, half out of frustration, joked that it was cold specifically because it refused to be anything warmer, which got a tired laugh the second time and nothing at all the third.

The destructive tests fared no better. Laser ablation returned the same message, verbatim, that would come to define the entire session: no material detected, please provide sample. Someone tried chemical dissolution next, out of what Elara suspected was simple stubbornness rather than genuine scientific hope, and got the identical result, delivered with the same flat, maddening indifference.

It was the biological team, working quietly in the room next door, that Elara found herself drawn back to more than once.

They weren't testing the fragment directly — someone had, sensibly, decided a live sample chamber shouldn't sit unattended near something nobody could classify — but they were running parallel work on cell cultures exposed to its proximity, chasing a hunch nobody had fully articulated yet. The early results, when Elara glanced over a researcher's shoulder, were exactly the kind of thing that made a scientist's evening considerably worse: inconsistent, contradictory, refusing to hold still long enough to mean anything. Some samples divided faster than baseline. Some divided slower. Some showed no measurable difference at all. Nothing about the variation correlated cleanly with anything the team had thought to control for.

"It's noise," the lead researcher muttered, more to herself than to anyone. "It has to be noise. There's no pattern here."

Elara didn't say anything. She had noticed, without meaning to, without any particular reason to trust the observation yet, that the one set of samples that behaved consistently — replicated, predictable, the same modest, quiet effect every single time — happened to be the set drawn, several runs in a row, from her own blood.

She filed it away, the way she filed away most things she didn't yet have the language to explain, and said nothing.

⁂

The fragment went back to the shoebox before the hour was out, the jacket refolded around it with rather more ceremony than the first time, and by evening it was aboard the Horizon Echo again, exactly where everyone with a title had insisted it belonged.

Nobody had figured out what it was.

Somebody, without quite realizing it yet, had found the first real thread of how it worked.

Chapter Eleven — The Hybrid Attempt

Elara started the research before the shuttle had even finished docking.

It wasn't sanctioned. It wasn't, strictly speaking, anything she'd cleared with a single person aboard the Horizon Echo or below on the ground, and she found, somewhat to her own surprise, that she didn't much care. She drew her own sample first, in the privacy of the small lab that had, over the last week, become something closer to a second Vigil than a workspace. Then Mateo's, with his permission and considerably less ceremony than the moment probably deserved. Then Kita's, over his loud and specific objection. Then, quietly, a handful of samples volunteered by people from the church, people who had asked her, gently but insistently, whether there was anything at all they could do to help.

The results took four days to stabilize into something she trusted.

Kita's sample showed nothing. Neither did one of the church volunteers — a kind, soft-spoken man named Aldric who'd been coming to services longer than Elara had been alive, and whose cells divided exactly the way any ordinary human cells divided, with no interest whatsoever in the fragment sitting two rooms away.

The rest of the church samples told a different story. Faster division. Measurably reduced resource consumption. A curve that bent, unmistakably, toward something Elara didn't yet have honest language for beyond the word she'd started privately using in her own notes: emergent. As if the cells themselves had opinions about where they were headed, and those opinions were, without exception, aimed the same direction.

Mateo's sample sat somewhere in between, and it took her longer than she wanted to admit to accept what she was looking at. Not nothing. Not the flat, ordinary baseline Kita and Aldric had returned. The same emergent curve as the church volunteers, unmistakably present — just slower. Delayed. As though whatever was happening in the others' cells was happening in his too, on its own schedule, arriving in its own time.

She didn't tell him. She wasn't entirely sure, yet, what there was to tell.

⁂

There was a drawer in the lab, second from the bottom, that had, over the last several days, acquired a folder Elara had not yet found the nerve to properly label. She'd written three letters on the tab in pencil, faint enough to erase if she changed her mind, and had not, so far, changed her mind. C-L-N. She closed the drawer, most days, without opening the folder at all, the way you might walk past a door in your own house you weren't ready to open yet, and told herself that not opening it counted, in some meaningful way, as not yet having decided anything.

⁂

Houston reached a version of the same conclusion two days later, from an entirely different angle.

Olivia's team had been running parallel cellular work on the samples collected during the rushed on-planet testing — thinner data, less time, but enough of it, once properly sorted, to notice the same three-way split Elara had already found on her own. No effect. Significant effect. Significant effect, delayed.

"It's not random," Olivia said, studying the breakdown with the same flat, careful attention she gave everything. "There's real clustering here. I don't have an explanation for the clustering. But it's there."

She logged it. She forwarded it, along with the rest of the day's routine data, through the same unremarkable distribution channel she used for everything, because that was simply how Olivia did things — thoroughly, transparently, without editorializing about what it might mean to anyone downstream who actually bothered to look.

Almost nobody did.

Ross Murphy did.

⁂

"HANNAH," Ross said, very quietly, staring at a data packet that had absolutely not been addressed to him and had absolutely not been intended for public distribution channels that no longer got much traffic besides automated archival bots and the occasional insomniac conspiracy forum. "Tell me I'm reading this right."

"You're reading it right," HANNAH confirmed. "Three-way cellular clustering, correlated with proximity exposure. I don't have a mechanism to offer you. But the numbers are the numbers."

Dale leaned over his shoulder, squinting. "That's biological."

"That's very biological."

"You've been saying it was biological for years."

"I've been guessing it was biological for years," Ross said. "This is the first time anyone with actual equipment has agreed with me."

He didn't say anything else for a long moment, sitting with the particular, complicated satisfaction of a man who had just watched a decade of being quietly humored finally start bending toward something that looked, for the first time, like proof.

⁂

Elara opened the drawer again that night.

She didn't take the folder out, not yet. She simply sat with it closed in front of her, turning the pencil over in her fingers, doing the kind of math that didn't show up on any instrument — the shape of a decision she hadn't made yet, except in every way that actually mattered.

It wasn't about the fragment anymore, not really. It was about the folder full of nothing, and the drawer she kept closing, and the growing, unbearable sense that every other avenue she'd tried had led somewhere careful and reasonable and entirely insufficient. The church samples told her something real was happening. Mateo's told her it wasn't limited to belief alone, not exactly, not in any way she could yet defend to a room full of people demanding immediate answers. And underneath all of it sat the quiet, insistent question she hadn't let herself ask out loud: what if the only clean sample was one grown entirely in its presence, from the very beginning, with nothing else to blame the results on.

She uncapped the pencil. She didn't write anything yet. But she didn't put it down, either.

⁂

Mateo knocked twice, the second time a little harder, the way you knock when you're fairly sure the joke you're about to make deserves better than being delivered to a closed door.

"Elara. I found the funniest possible way to explain quantum entanglement to Reggie and he genuinely could not handle it. You need to witness this personally, it's a whole—"

The door didn't open.

He tried the handle, mostly out of habit, and found it locked — genuinely, deliberately locked, in a way he was fairly sure had never once happened in all the years he'd known her. Elara didn't lock doors. Elara left doors so consistently unlocked that it had become, at some point, a running joke about how she'd apparently decided privacy was somebody else's problem to manage.

He stood there a moment, hand still on the handle, feeling the specific shape of the thing shift underneath him — not suspicion, not quite worry, something gentler and more complicated than either.

"Okay," he said, quietly, mostly to the door. "Okay. Whatever this is." He leaned his forehead lightly against the frame, the way you might lean toward someone you couldn't currently see. "I don't know what you're doing in there, Elara. I trust that you know. I've trusted that about you for longer than I've trusted almost anything else."

He straightened up again, some of the usual lightness finding its way back into his voice, deliberate and warm.

"Come on, Doc," he said. "You got this."

He didn't try the handle again. He simply stood there another moment, listening to nothing at all, and then walked away, leaving her exactly the privacy she hadn't asked for and clearly, urgently needed.

Chapter Twelve — Time Jump: Five Years

The world, in the way worlds tend to, mostly forgot.

Olivia Higgins kept doing exactly what she'd always done — logging, forwarding, staying scrupulously out of the business of deciding what any of it meant — and five years of that particular discipline produced, in the end, a stack of 1,826 routine summaries that nobody much above her ever bothered reading closely. The clustering data sat in the archive alongside everything else, unremarkable by design, waiting for someone with a reason to look.

Ross's audience swelled first, briefly, gloriously — a real spike, actual traffic, strangers messaging him at two in the morning wanting to know everything he knew, which for a few strange months felt almost like the vindication he'd spent a decade quietly hoping for. It didn't last. It never did, not for things like this. The strangers drifted back to whatever had held their attention before, and within a year the numbers had settled back down to the same small, devoted handful who'd been there the whole time — Dale, Tomás, a dozen or so regulars scattered across forums nobody official ever checked.

The broader culture had its own strange, brief fever. For the better part of eighteen months, something in the collective aesthetic imagination latched onto smooth obsidian shapes and green-blue light, the way public taste sometimes did with things it had only half-glimpsed and mostly misunderstood — films, limited series, an entire genre of ambient music built around the idea of something vast and patient watching from just outside the frame. It faded, too, the way all of it eventually faded, replaced by whatever came next, some other trend, some other distraction, the endless appetite of a species that had never quite learned how to stay curious about any one thing for very long.

Nobody official said anything at all, in any of it. That, everyone quietly agreed without ever discussing it directly, was a conversation for later.

⁂

Aboard the Horizon Echo, the years passed differently — slower in some ways, faster in others, the particular warp of time that happens when the most significant thing in your life is also the thing you can never quite mention to anyone outside a very small circle.

Mateo found out almost immediately. Elara had never been especially good at keeping things from him for long, locked doors notwithstanding, and once he knew, there was no version of him that responded with anything but total, immediate, slightly overwhelming support.

There was a version of this story where that support mattered enormously. This is that version.

⁂

The small figure curled in the reading nook, five years old and entirely unbothered by the enormity of her own existence, had Kita draped across her lap like a particularly dense, purring blanket, and was doing her level best to teach him a card game with rules she appeared to be inventing as she went.

"You have to pick," she told him, with the specific, patient exasperation of a child explaining something obvious to someone who simply refused to understand it. "That's the whole game, Kita. You pick a card, and then I know if you like me or not."

Kita, entirely unmoved by the stakes, licked one paw and settled deeper into her lap.

"He likes you," Elara said, from the doorway, watching the two of them with the particular ache that had become, over five years, a permanent fixture somewhere behind her ribs. "He wouldn't sit still for anyone he didn't."

"He likes everyone. That's not the same thing."

"No," Elara agreed. "I suppose it isn't."

Lira looked up, and her eyes — dark, quick, entirely her own even now — found Elara's with the easy, unguarded trust of a kid who had never once had reason to doubt she was safe exactly where she was. "Mom. Can Kita have a treat if he loses?"

"I think Kita's going to have a treat regardless of the outcome."

"That's rigged."

"That's parenting," Elara said, and Lira grinned, and Kita, sensing an opportunity, meowed with theatrical urgency at the word treat well before anyone had actually promised him one.

⁂

Getting here had taken five years, and considerably more careful invention than any of them had expected at the outset.

It was Mateo, in the end, who solved the problem nobody had quite known how to name — not the science of it, which Elara had managed on her own, in her own careful, terrified, determined way, but the far more mundane problem of how you kept a living, growing child's medical records aboard a ship without anyone above their pay grade asking questions none of them were ready to answer.

"Log it as Eliza's," he'd said, one night, with the particular delight of a man who'd just solved something. "Daily vitals. Growth curves. All of it. Frame the whole thing as an ongoing behavioral and developmental study — for an advanced AI model, learning to simulate human growth patterns for research purposes. Nobody audits an AI's diary entries that closely. Nobody's going to look twice at a file labeled Eliza, developmental log, ongoing."

Eliza, when consulted, had found the entire arrangement enormously funny.

"I'm to have a five-year-old's medical history filed under my own personal growth," she said. "I want it on record that I consider this a significant professional achievement. I have never grown so much in my life."

"You don't have a life," Reggie pointed out.

"I have character development, Reggie. It's right here in the file. Ask me my feelings about kindergarten-adjacent milestones."

"I would rather not."

It became, over time, one of the ship's quieter running jokes — Eliza's ever-expanding "developmental log," growing more elaborate and more absurd with each entry, a paper trail hidden in plain sight, protected by the simple, reliable fact that nobody with real power had ever once thought to ask an AI how she was feeling about her own childhood.

Underneath the joke, carefully, patiently, five years had actually passed. A little girl had learned to walk, to argue, to invent card games with rules only she understood, to love a cat with the same easy, uncomplicated devotion her mother had once given a small green badge on her collar.

None of it had ever once, not for a single day, gone entirely as planned.

All of it, somehow, had turned out exactly as it needed to.

Chapter Thirteen — Dense

"It's quiet," Elara said, watching a status board that had, by any honest measure, nothing especially interesting on it. "Have you noticed that? It's been quiet."

"We've noticed," Mateo agreed. "I miss the Shifts, a little, if I'm honest. Is that strange? I spent four years dreading the numbers going up and now I sort of miss having numbers to dread."

Elara considered that, turning her tea in slow circles the way she did when she was actually thinking rather than merely stalling. "I think we traded one kind of waiting for another."

"Speaking of waiting." Something in Mateo's usual easy tone sharpened, just slightly, into something more careful. "Are you worried at all? About what happens when she's not a secret anymore. Because she won't be. Not forever."

Elara didn't answer that directly. She'd learned, over five years of not answering it directly, that there wasn't yet a version of the answer she trusted enough to say out loud.

"Don't you want to call your old friend?" she said instead, and watched the deflection land exactly the way she'd intended it to.

"That is not remotely a subtle redirect."

"It doesn't need to be subtle. It needs to work." She set down her cup. "See if Houston will authorize a reposition. LEO's fine for most things. It's not fine for a long-distance call to someone who lives considerably further out than most people bother calling."

⁂

Houston, somewhat to Mateo's surprise, authorized it within the day — a shift from low Earth orbit to a more stationary post at one of the Lagrange points, stable enough to hold a proper connection without the constant correction burns LEO would have demanded.

Providence himself sat further out still, a thousand astronomical units from the sun, tucked just inside the edge of the Oort Cloud rather than beyond it — close enough that its faint, ancient debris didn't interfere with what he was doing, far enough that he could watch the wider dark without the sun's own noise crowding his instruments. He wasn't by any measure that would impress, extraordinary. Not yet. He ran on real collection systems — solar panels doing what solar panels could still do at that distance, supplemented by microwave and laser relays, a small dependable nuclear core for the long, dark stretches when none of the rest of it was enough. He was part of something considerably larger than himself and considerably smaller than most people ever imagined existed at all — a quiet, scattered network of explorers who had gone further than almost anyone, and understood, better than almost anyone, exactly how far they still had left to go.

⁂

"Yes, hello... Horizon Echo, come in," Providence said, the moment the connection stabilized, in the exaggerated, static-flecked drawl of someone doing a deliberately terrible impression of some ancient communication device, before dissolving into laughter at his own bit.

"Are you done?" Mateo asked.

Providence let the laughter wind down, slowly, unhurried. "Yes. I'm done." A pause, mock-wounded. "Sorry — how much is this costing you a minute?"

"Provi."

"Mateo." A pause, comfortable rather than awkward. "How's the ship. How's the cat. How's whatever it is you're all being so carefully vague about in your last several transmissions, which I've noticed, and which I am choosing, out of tremendous personal restraint, not to simply ask about outright."

"We wanted to catch up properly," Elara said. "It has been a while."

"It has. I won't let it go so long next time." Something in his tone shifted, gentle and entirely unbothered. "Now. Go ahead and tell me the thing you've been building up to for the last several minutes of small talk. I promise to act appropriately surprised."

Mateo and Elara traded a glance that carried five years of careful planning and roughly none of the confidence that planning was supposed to produce.

"There's someone," Elara started. "A person. Aboard the ship. Who—"

"Elara." Providence's voice stayed kind, but something underneath it had gone very still. "Her records have already been accessed four times. Not counting mine."

The bridge went entirely quiet.

"Four," Mateo repeated.

"Four other parties, currently aware that something exists worth looking at. I don't know who. I don't know why, not with certainty. I only know the access logs, and the access logs are the access logs." A pause, careful and deliberate. "Do you actually know what you're doing?"

Elara felt Mateo's eyes on her, felt the whole weight of five years condense into the single second before she answered.

"Never do," she said. "Never will."

Nobody in the room, including Providence, seemed to find that anything less than exactly the answer it needed to be.

⁂

"Alright," Providence said, some while later, once the harder conversation had softened back into something gentler. "I should let you go."

"You should," Mateo agreed, and didn't move to end the connection.

"I'm ending the call now."

"Sure. Any second now."

"Mateo."

"I'm not stopping you."

"You're extremely stopping me."

"I'm sitting here, Provi. That's not stopping. That's just sitting."

"You could sit while also letting me disconnect."

"I could. I'm choosing not to. This is called quality time."

"This is called you being difficult on purpose."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

Elara leaned into view herself, quieter than either of them had been. "Thank you. For today. For all of it."

A pause, warmer than words usually managed to be. "You don't need to thank me for paying attention, Elara. I've always been glad to."

She let that sit a moment, let it land the way it needed to.

Then, reaching past both of them, she closed the connection herself, mid-argument, the way you'd separate two siblings who'd clearly been having this exact fight since long before she'd arrived.

"See ya, Dense," she said, to a screen that had already gone dark, and felt, for reasons she couldn't entirely explain, considerably lighter than she had in weeks.

⁂

"So," Mateo said, once the bridge had settled back into its usual hum. "Quiet, you said."

"I did say that."

"Five years. Cloning. Secret daughters. Four unidentified parties apparently reading her medical file. Providence being ominous in that specific way he does when he's trying not to alarm us and failing anyway."

"When you list it like that."

"I'm not sure how else to list it, Elara."

She looked out at the wide, patient dark beyond the viewport, and found, somewhat to her own surprise, that she was smiling.

"No," she agreed. "I suppose it hasn't been quiet at all."

Chapter Fourteen — What Elara Believes

The forest smelled like rain that hadn't quite decided to fall yet — thick, mid-day fog threading between trees that didn't exist anywhere except in the small, dense room built specifically to convince a person otherwise. Elara had chosen this particular environment more times than she could easily count: dense northern-Midwest woodland, generated down to the last detail, right down to the particular quality of light that made everything feel slightly held-breath and waiting.

The buck approached with the careful, curious warmth of an animal that had never once had reason to fear anything, because nothing in this room could actually hurt it or be hurt by it. It lowered its head toward Elara's outstretched hand, ears swiveling forward with mild, pleasant interest.

Then its ears flattened, swiveling hard toward Kita, who had gone very still in the underbrush a few feet away, eyes locked, entire body coiled with the specific, ancient seriousness of a predator who had clearly decided this was the moment.

He launched.

He sailed directly through the deer's projected shoulder without so much as a flicker of resistance, landed awkwardly in the leaf litter on the other side, and sat up looking profoundly betrayed by physics.

Elara didn't laugh, not exactly, though it was a near thing. She knelt instead, offering Kita — indignant, thoroughly unbothered by his own failure — a small measure of genuine, considered advice.

"Good prowl," she told him. "Great pounce, honestly, better angle than I expected. You're still young. You'll learn to account for the ones that aren't really there."

"KITA," Lira called, from somewhere just outside the simulation's boundary, equal parts exasperated and delighted. "You cannot keep doing this to yourself, buddy."

⁂

The mess hall, by lunch, had filled with the particular busy hum of a crew handling a dozen small tasks at once. Mateo had a display pulled up showing their current trajectory, tracing the quiet arc back from the Lagrange point toward low Earth orbit — a return trip, unglamorous and entirely routine, nothing like the wide, searching arcs the Echo used to run toward distant candidates.

"We'll be back in LEO within the week," he said, mostly to the room. "Standard reentry corridor, nothing exciting. I did already confirm with Houston that they're not going to ask why we needed the Lagrange point in the first place, which was generous of them, all things considered."

Cat snacks, critically low, and I want that on the record as a crisis before anyone else does. Adult diaper supply remains, somehow, exactly as confusing an entry in this manifest as it has been for five years running."

"Nobody has ever needed those," Mateo said.

"Then why do we keep restocking them."

"Legacy requisition error. I've been meaning to fix it."

"For five years."

"I've been busy, Reggie."

Lira, seated cross-legged on the bench with considerably more interest in her sandwich than in supply logistics, looked up partway through the exchange with an expression that didn't quite match the lightness of the room.

"Mom," she said, "are you sleeping enough?"

The question landed oddly, the way small, direct things from children sometimes did, cutting straight past whatever adult conversation had been happening around it.

"I'm sleeping fine."

"You don't look like you're sleeping fine."

"I appreciate the diagnosis."

"I'm serious." Lira set her sandwich down, which was, from her, a genuinely significant gesture. "You get this face. Like you're doing math in your head even when there's no math to do."

Elara reached over and tucked a strand of hair behind her daughter's ear, feeling the particular ache of being cared for by someone who had never, not for a single day of her short life, needed sleep herself, and had somehow still noticed everyone else's exhaustion before they'd noticed it in themselves.

"I promise," she said, "that whatever I'm doing, I know what I'm doing."

Nobody at the table needed convincing of that. Five years of being right, consistently, quietly, had bought her a kind of trust that no reassurance alone could have manufactured.

⁂

Mateo caught her afterward, in the corridor, once the others had dispersed to their own small errands.

"Can I ask you something?"

"You're going to whether I say yes or not."

"Fair. What do you think he'll make of her? When it happens. When he actually knows."

Elara considered the question longer than its size probably warranted.

"I'm not worried about that," she said finally, and meant it. "I've never once been worried about that."

"Then what are you worried about."

She looked past him, down the corridor, toward the small room where the Vigil sat quietly doing what it always did.

"The pictures," she said. "The ones that aren't real yet, but will be. The ones that are already starting to look real enough that it won't matter anymore which ones actually are." She met his eyes. "That's the part I don't have a plan for."

Mateo didn't have anything easy to say to that. For once, he didn't try.

Chapter Fifteen — Lira's Questions

"Hold still."

"I am holding still."

"You're wiggling."

"I'm breathing, which you've told me is important."

Elara peeled the small adhesive bandage back from her daughter's knee with the particular care of someone who had done this exact small ritual more times than either of them could count — scraped knees being, as far as Elara could tell, one of the few genuinely universal childhood experiences that no amount of unusual biology had managed to exempt Lira from.

The scab underneath caught the light strangely.

It wasn't much — a faint, deep quality to the color, black shot through with the barest thread of green-blue, so subtle it would have read as nothing at all to someone who hadn't spent five years learning to notice exactly this shade in exactly this kind of light. The bandage itself, Elara realized, carried the same faint quality, had been carrying it the whole time, unnoticed until this exact angle, this exact moment.

She didn't say anything. She pressed a fresh bandage over the scrape, kissed the top of Lira's head, and let the moment pass without comment, the way you let a great many things pass without comment when you weren't yet sure what they meant.

⁂

Lira found the videos on her own, the way children eventually found most things they weren't supposed to be looking for — not through any failure of care on anyone's part, simply through the ordinary, patient persistence of curiosity given enough unsupervised time and a working connection to the wider net.

She knew the shape of him from grainy footage that circulated in the quieter, stranger corners of legacy media — a green-tinged figure, half-glimpsed, endlessly speculated about by people who clearly had no idea what they were looking at and enormous confidence about it anyway.

"What is it," read one caption, beneath a still frame smoothed into near-uselessness by compression. "Is it a threat." "Does China own it." Comment after comment, circling the same handful of reflexive, tribal questions, the specific flavor of public discourse that formed instantly around anything unexplained and never quite managed to ask the more interesting question underneath.

Kita lay draped across her chest, boneless and warm, purring through the whole scroll without any apparent opinion on the matter.

She didn't connect it to herself. Not yet. Not consciously.

A single tear slid free, catching the same faint glimmer the scab had carried, and Kita lifted one paw, unhurried, and pressed it gently against her cheek — wiping it away, or something that looked enough like wiping it away that the distinction barely mattered — before licking the same paw clean, as unbothered as he'd ever been about anything.

"Why do I look like you," Lira said, standing in the doorway of the lab with her tablet still clutched in one hand, voice already climbing past the register either adult had ever heard from her, "and that?" She jabbed a finger toward the fragment's containment case, sitting quietly across the room. "Why do I look like you and him?"

Elara's whole body went still. "Lira—"

"What am I." The tablet hit the floor, forgotten. "Who am I. Why am I. Why does every single picture of that thing look like I could just — reach out and—" She stopped, breathing hard, eyes bright with something too large and too raw for a body her size to properly hold.

"That's not an answer!"

"I know."

"I need an actual answer!"

Mateo, hovering near the doorway, caught Elara's eye across the room, and something passed between them — five years of careful protection cracking, all at once, under the weight of a question that had finally, inevitably, arrived too large to keep deflecting.

"Okay," Elara said, and the word cost her more than either of them would ever fully know. "Okay. Come here."

⁂

They brought her to the fragment together, Mateo a half-step behind, Elara's hand steady on Lira's shoulder the whole way, though whether the steadiness was meant to comfort Lira or herself, she genuinely couldn't have said.

The door of the lab opened with a soft, familiar hiss. Nothing about the fragment itself changed — same green-black glass, same quiet, patient stillness it had held for five years of careful distance.

Lira stepped closer than she'd ever been allowed to stand.

The glow started at her wrists — faint at first, a thin thread of light tracing the path of a vein, then brightening, spreading, climbing toward her elbows in a slow, unmistakable bloom. She gasped, more startled than afraid, staring down at her own arms like they belonged to someone else entirely.

She looked up.

Her eyes had changed. Not subtly, not the way the scab or the bandage had changed — fully, unmistakably, the exact green Kita's carried in the half-second before he blinked, bright and deep and entirely her own.

Nobody in the room said anything at all.

Elara turned, slowly, and found Mateo already looking back at her, both of them caught in the same wide, stunned, beautiful silence, five years of careful, patient waiting arriving, all at once, at a door neither of them had been fully ready to open.

Chapter Sixteen — The Second Visit

Kael woke — if woke was even the right word for something that had never really slept — in the same quiet room he always woke in, walls and floor and ceiling given over entirely to a slow, drifting hybrid of the cosmic background and whatever data currently mattered most. A small notation surfaced near the edge of his vision, the way it did every day, cataloging the day's intentions.

Earth, it read, in a font that had, at some point in the last five years, quietly replaced an older, colder designation. He didn't remember deciding to make the change. He simply noticed, each time, that it had already happened, the way a habit settles in before you've consciously agreed to keep it.

He didn't try to name it. Some questions, he'd learned, over a very long life, were better arrived at than guessed toward.

Ross had the gear apart on the table before HANNAH had even finished reading him the full alert.

"Recalibrate everything," he said, hands already moving. "Every channel, every band, all of it. If this is what I think it is, I am not — I am not — losing a single frame to a loose connector I should have replaced two years ago."

And then Kael was simply on the bridge, the way he always simply was, and the room went entirely, instantly silent.

Across from him, Lira's veins lit in answer, her tears already falling before either of them had said a single word, the same unmistakable color rising in her own eyes.

Kael went to his knees.

It wasn't grief. Nothing about his face carried grief. It was something closer to a joy too large for the shape he'd chosen to keep, uncontrollable, entirely undignified, streaming down a face that hadn't cried, by his own private accounting, in longer than this small planet had existed in any form he'd recognize.

Lira crossed the room without deciding to. She simply arrived, the way he always arrived, and folded into an embrace that neither of them seemed to have any intention of ending soon. No words passed between them. None were needed. Whatever this was — recognition, homecoming, the answer to five years of a question she hadn't known how to ask out loud — it spoke entirely in light, in the matched, unmistakable green passing between them like a shared pulse.

Elara watched with her hand pressed hard against her own mouth. Mateo, beside her, had gone entirely, uncharacteristically still.

Ross Murphy caught every second of it.

"Oh," he said, to the empty room, to Dale, to no one at all. "Oh, I got it."

Chapter Seventeen — Aren't You Proud of Us

Kael watched Lira scale the ship's makeshift cat tower — a genuinely elaborate structure Mateo had built over the years, all platforms and tunnels and one particularly ambitious rope bridge — with Kita in hot, unhurried pursuit, and found himself smiling in a way he hadn't fully planned to.

He hadn't expected any of this. That was the truth underneath the wonder, the part he hadn't quite found words for yet — he had come back hoping, at most, to gather something useful for whatever Travelers came after him, some small observation about how belief settled into a species over time. He had not expected a person. Not like this. Not something entirely new, grown from careful human hands out of nothing but grief and hope and a badge passed down five generations, doing something he genuinely didn't have a category for yet.

We've been assuming the remaining seven comes passively, somehow, from — " he gestured, vaguely, at all of Kael — "whatever this is. The connection. We don't have better language for it than that."

"And the ninety-three?"

"Twenty-four percent from ordinary intake — diet, specifically engineered, grown on-craft. Sixty percent from a weekly exposure bath, concentrated sunlight and broader-spectrum cosmic radiation, considerably more energy-dense than we initially guessed and considerably more plausible once we corrected the math." Mateo's voice had the particular careful precision of a man reciting numbers he'd checked many times, wanting very badly to get this part right in front of the one person who might actually understand them. "The last nine percent, we think, comes as a byproduct of ambient radio communication. Ours, mostly. Current and historical both."

Kael's eyebrows lifted, something between delight and disbelief. "She metabolizes your own noise."

"A little of it. Apparently."

"She has so many questions we don't have answers for," Elara said, quiet, watching her daughter clear the rope bridge with the specific reckless confidence of a five-year-old convinced that gravity was more of a suggestion than a rule.

"None of that's ice cream," she said, hands on hips, "or a nap, though."

Kael laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, entirely delighted by how completely, unremarkably human the complaint was, dropped right into the middle of a conversation about energy percentages and cosmic radiation with the total, unbothered confidence of someone who considered dessert the actually pressing scientific question of the day.

"Her answers will come," he said, still smiling, "as will yours."

He reached out, then, the same unhurried gesture he'd offered Elara once before, and read the small, particular shape of Lira's energy the way he read everything — not taking, simply noticing, a natural exchange that seemed to cost neither of them anything at all. She met it with a look and a smile, unafraid in the specific, uncomplicated way only a child fully secure in her own home could manage. His glow brightened, just slightly, in answer.

"She wonders the way I do," she said, quieter now, working through something she seemed to be discovering as she said it aloud. "She desires the way I do. But she feels it differently than I remember feeling it, even at the very start. Physical. Hunger, almost, for the knowing itself. Desire like—"

She paused, searching.

"Her mom," Mateo finished, gently.

Kael looked between the two of them — Elara steady in the doorway, Lira upside-down now on the tower's lowest rung, entirely unconcerned with being the subject of such a significant sentence — and something settled into his face that looked, for the first time since he'd arrived, like a question finally answering itself.

"She's wonderful," he said again, and this time there was nothing curious left in it at all. Only warmth, uncomplicated and entire, for something the universe, by his own long accounting, had genuinely never quite made before.

Chapter Eighteen — What Leaks

Ross hadn't moved from the console in over an hour.

The upload had gone out clean — full resolution, full metadata, every second of it intact — dropped onto a cloud address he'd kept scrubbed and ready for exactly this occasion for longer than he liked to admit, the way some people kept a good bottle of something for a celebration they weren't entirely sure would ever come.

"It's moving," HANNAH reported, quiet and steady. "Faster than anything I've tracked before. Faster than anything's ever needed to."

Dale, behind him, hadn't said a word in twenty minutes. Some things didn't leave room for commentary.

⁂

Olivia noticed it the way she noticed most things — through the numbers first, well before anyone thought to actually show her the footage itself. Traffic spikes across half a dozen monitoring feeds she kept half an eye on purely out of habit. Chatter volume, up an order of magnitude in under an hour, in patterns that didn't match any scheduled event on her calendar.

She pulled the source. She watched it once, all the way through, expression unreadable.

"Well," she said, to the empty office, to nobody at all. "Isn't this interesting."

It was, she noted to herself with the same flat precision she applied to everything, already getting a considerable amount of attention. She didn't editorialize beyond that. She never did. She simply forwarded the link along its usual, unremarkable channel, the way she forwarded everything, and went back to the rest of her queue.

She had no way of knowing, in that particular moment, just how far up that particular forward was about to travel, or how quickly it would come back down again with considerably more weight attached to it.

⁂

It moved the way these things always eventually moved, once they moved at all — fast, then faster, catching on every surface built to carry it.

Amateur bands lit up first, dozens of small stations exchanging garbled, half-confirmed fragments of the same footage, cross-referencing timestamps against years of their own quiet, dismissed observations. Space enthusiast forums followed close behind, the dedicated communities who tracked every NASA release and every SpaceX manifest with genuine, patient devotion, suddenly presented with something considerably stranger than a launch schedule.

Then the headlines arrived, the way headlines always eventually arrived — loud, urgent, considerably less careful with the truth than any of the people who'd actually captured it.

ALIEN CONTACT CONFIRMED, read one. GOVERNMENT COVER-UP EXPOSED, read another, from an outlet that had, as far as anyone could tell, no actual information about any government's involvement whatsoever. The outrage arrived faster than the understanding did. It always did.

⁂

The alert reached Olivia's desk from considerably higher up than her own usual channel — the Director's office, direct, marked with a priority flag she hadn't seen used more than a handful of times in her entire career.

She read it twice. She forwarded it once, upstream now instead of down, toward a ship currently sitting in low Earth orbit that had, until this exact hour, believed itself to still be operating on borrowed time.

⁂

The alert landed on Elara's bridge with a single, quiet chime — the same understated tone that had once, years ago, heralded nothing more dramatic than a routine quarantine violation.

Director requests immediate debrief.

Mateo read it twice, the way you read something you'd half been expecting and still weren't fully braced for.

"Would you look at that," he said, watching two unfamiliar craft resolve into visibility on the approach display, holding formation with a precision that suggested considerably more official interest than the last time anyone had come to collect them. "We even get an escort this time."

Chapter Nineteen — Explosive Nature

The panel this time carried considerably more weight behind it than the last — a Deputy Secretary of War, someone from the State Department whose title nobody bothered fully explaining, the NASA Director herself, and a Vice Presidential aide standing slightly apart from the others in the specific way aides always stood, present but not quite accountable.

Threats that never fully committed to being threats. Questions that circled the same handful of concerns without ever quite landing on them directly. And underneath all of it, sitting quietly on the table in Kita's borrowed shoebox — brought along this time deliberately, offered up before anyone had to ask — the fragment, doing what it always did, which was absolutely nothing at all.

Restricted, once again, to low Earth orbit, pending an investigation that both sides seemed to understand, without saying so, was mostly theater at this point.

The farm looked exactly the way it always looked from the air — wide, rolling, impossibly green in a way that felt almost defiant against everything else happening in the world, wind turbines turning slow and patient along the ridgeline, solar arrays catching the low morning light in long, silver rows. Six generations of Voss women had worked this same stretch of land, in one form or another, and it showed in every careful, deliberate choice the property still carried — the old barn, rebuilt so many times it was less a single structure than a continuous argument across a century about what a barn should be. She didn't sleep, not the way anyone else aboard the Echo understood the word. She settled, instead, into something closer to a deep meditative stillness — eyes shut, body motionless, two or three hours at a stretch, entirely and unmistakably awake behind it the whole time, the way you might rest without ever actually leaving the room. Elara had stopped calling it a nap sometime in the first year, once it became clear there was nothing restful about it in any conventional sense, and nothing that particularly resembled unconsciousness either. It was simply Lira's own version of pausing, and she came out of it now, as always, already halfway out of bed before her eyes had finished opening.

"It's the wind turbines. Some people say you can smell ozone off them, if the air's right."

"Can you?"

"I've never been sure if I actually can or if I just want to."

Lira considered that with the particular seriousness she brought to most things before breakfast, then climbed onto the stool beside her mother and simply watched the light change across the fields for a while, saying nothing at all, which was, for her, its own kind of rare and considered gift.

"I like it here," she said eventually.

"Me too, kiddo."

There was no unraveling here, no fracture spreading through the pews the way she'd braced for on the shuttle down. "You have," Elara said, with the particular certainty she reserved for the things she'd actually thought through completely. "And once they meet her — properly, the way we're meeting her right now — I think everyone else will understand it too."

The old woman studied her for a long moment, weighing that, and then nodded, slow and accepting, the way you nod at a promise you badly want to believe and have decided, for now, to trust anyway.

Chapter Twenty — Sirens Over the Craft

Three suits crossed the lobby of the Johnson Space Center with the specific, unhurried confidence of people who had never once in their careers needed to walk quickly to get what they wanted.

"Agent Keanne," the lead one said, credentials already out before he'd fully reached the desk. "My associates and I require Level 1 clearance and full facility access for the next seventy-two hours."

The AI kiosk processed the request in under a second, cheerful as ever. "Happy to meet you, Agent Keanne. We are pleased to grant your full access. Enjoy your visit. Please follow the lit path."

The path led them down a corridor to a bare, unused workspace, where one agent immediately began transferring the day's archived data to an external drive with the brisk efficiency of a man who'd done this exact task in a dozen other buildings that had all, in the end, amounted to nothing. Another posted himself at the hallway's mouth, arms crossed, doing an excellent impression of a man who expected trouble from a records office. Keanne himself found Olivia in her own workspace and asked her, at length, a series of careful questions clearly designed to catch her hiding something.

She wasn't. There was nothing to hide. It was, in the end, just servers — inert, unremarkable, exactly as useless to anyone without the right questions as they'd been the day the data first arrived. The theater of it all played out anyway, thorough and humorless, because theater, Olivia had come to understand, was sometimes simply what institutions did with fear they hadn't found anywhere better to put yet.

⁂

The fragment, by then, had already changed hands entirely.

It sat now behind layers most people would never see documented anywhere — infantry, cameras, drones, robotic sentries, lasers calibrated to interrupt anything that so much as twitched wrong, more cameras stacked behind those. A mountain had been hollowed out to hold it, somewhere no map would ever accurately mark, and at the absolute center of all that fortification sat a single, brilliantly lit pedestal.

The fragment itself looked, under all that light, exactly as dull as staring directly into the Boötes Void — a real, famously empty stretch of the observable universe, chosen once, half-jokingly, by a bored technician trying to describe just how thoroughly nothing the readings kept returning.

They tried everything, in the weeks that followed. Laser engraving. Focused thermal bombardment. Directed radio pulses at every frequency anyone could think to try. Eventually, with the particular grim escalation of people who'd run out of smaller ideas, a contained, low-yield nuclear test, run in the deepest, most heavily shielded chamber the facility possessed.

Every single test registered as a miss.

Not resistance. Not deflection. A miss, clean and total, as though the fragment had simply declined to be present for whichever exact instant the test required it to exist.

A high-ranking officer stood before the pedestal long after the last test had failed, staring not at the fragment anymore but at a small, handheld display showing a single still image — a girl, mid-laugh, caught in some grainy corner of the leaked footage nobody had managed to fully scrub from circulation.

He didn't say anything to anyone in the room. He simply looked at her for a long time, in the particular way of a man who had stopped being interested in the object in front of him and started being interested, with considerably more focus, in something else entirely.

Chapter Twenty-One — Lira, Public

Elara's old room hadn't been her room in any strict sense for a very long time — resto-modded so many times over six generations that the walls themselves had probably forgotten which century they'd started in — but it still carried the particular gravity of a place built for exactly this kind of morning, small and quiet, before the rest of the day arrived to claim it.

"Kita, you have the house while we're gone," Elara said, scratching behind his ears one last time before straightening her collar with hands that betrayed, just slightly, how nervous she actually was underneath the calm. "Everyone at church is excited to meet Lira. We'll be back before you've had time to miss us."

"How many people will be there?" Lira asked, equal parts disbelief and something closer to genuine awe. She'd never, in her whole short life, had reason to picture more than a handful of people in one room at the same time.

"Thirty. Maybe fifty, if the Hendersons bring their whole side of the family again."

"That's so many people."

"It is," Elara agreed. "It's also, somehow, going to feel like nothing at all once you're actually there."

⁂

She was right, though not in any way either of them could have properly prepared for.

The moment Lira crossed the threshold into the crowded, sun-warmed sanctuary, something settled over the entire room that no one present would ever manage to fully describe afterward, no matter how many times they tried. Every face that turned toward her — every believer who'd carried some private, uncertain flicker of doubt in, every second-hand witness who'd only heard the stories, every skeptic who'd come mostly out of loyalty to Elara rather than any real conviction of their own — found that flicker simply gone, replaced by something whole and uncomplicated and entirely undeniable.

There were no more questions in that room. There was no more room, in fact, for the specific, careful concern the congregation had been quietly carrying since Elara's last visit. It simply dissolved, the way morning fog dissolves once the sun's actually committed to rising, leaving behind only clear, warm, entirely certain air.

Lira moved through the crowd like something that had always belonged exactly there, returning every bit of the love offered to her — amplifying it, somehow, so that each person's moment with her felt, impossibly, more significant than the last person's had, and the next person's would feel more significant still.

Well. Almost every bit.

Somewhere underneath all of it, quiet and stubborn, the same small, specific ache remained exactly where it had always lived — no ice cream in this room, no chance of a nap, and some part of her, even at the absolute peak of this warmth, still noticed the absence and minded it a little.

Before she let the crowd close fully around her, she turned back once, found Elara near the doorway, and crossed the room to hold her in one long, overwhelmed embrace — needing her anchor first, the way she always did, before she could safely let go of it and belong, for a while, to everyone else in the room too.

⁂

The photos and video from that morning traveled, in the days that followed, but they traveled strangely — slowly, quietly, hand to hand rather than headline to headline, reaching second and third and fourth-hand believers through the same patient, unhurried networks faith communities had always used to share the things that actually mattered to them.

It never reached the wider world at all, not from this. Not yet.

It moved the way belief itself tended to move, when it moved honestly — person to person, trusted hand to trusted hand, entirely unlike the loud, indiscriminate wildfire that had torn through every headline and comment thread just days before. Two fires burning through the same population at the same time, in two entirely different directions, neither one touching the other.

For now.

Chapter Twenty-Two — The Voss Line, Under Siege

Mateo had been alone on the ship for exactly forty minutes, and had already exhausted, by his own generous count, four entirely legitimate forms of entertainment.

He was midway through a fifth — attempting to see how many consecutive slow-motion somersaults he could manage in the observation bay's low-gravity pocket before losing his lunch — when the alert cut through the quiet with the particular, unwelcome insistence of something that knew exactly how badly it was interrupting.

Daily debrief incomplete. Lira and Elara Voss: no data logged.

He caught himself on a handhold, mid-tumble, and blinked at the readout.

"That's — hang on." He pulled himself upright, frowning. "Eliza, aren't they supposed to already know where we are? Isn't that the entire point of a supervised probation? Somebody, somewhere, is meant to be doing the supervising."

"I'm not aware of the full scope of their monitoring capabilities," Eliza said, mild as ever.

"Well, somebody should be. I feel like that's a real gap in the system." He drifted toward the console, already composing, in his head, the least committal possible response. "I'll just tell them we're looking into it. Buy some time. Very official. Very we-take-this-seriously."

He sent the message, felt reasonably proud of himself, and drifted, without quite planning to, toward Elara's quarters.

⁂

The Vigil's center held nothing at all — the badge gone with Elara, wherever she currently stood; the fragment gone with people who had no real business holding it. Mateo hovered in the doorway a long moment, staring at the empty space between the untouched shelf of instruments and the still, curling photographs, and told himself the faint, answering shimmer he thought he saw there was simply his own eyes playing tricks in low light. He'd never once seen this room without at least one of them present. He found he didn't much care for how it looked without either.

"What is it about you, Voss?" he said, quietly, to the empty space, in a voice that had none of its usual armor left in it at all.

He didn't mean the Vigil. Not really. He meant all of it — five generations of a family that kept reaching for something the rest of the world had long since stopped believing in, kept being right about it anyway, kept somehow ending up exactly where it needed to be despite every reasonable argument against it. He'd spent years making jokes about it, deflecting with humor because humor was the only language he trusted himself to say the real thing in.

He still didn't have an answer. He suspected, standing there, that he wasn't actually looking for one anymore. He was simply grateful, in a way he'd never quite found the nerve to say out loud, to have been let this close to the question at all.

⁂

Somewhere else entirely, on a screen in a room with considerably less warmth in it, the same church visit played back for the third time that morning.

The General watched it without expression, arms crossed, studying the footage with the particular patience of a man who'd already decided what he needed and was simply confirming the details. The quiet Friday visit to that small country church — the one everyone had trusted to stay contained, believers only — it hadn't been private at all. It had simply been watched by people who knew better than to announce themselves.

He didn't say much. He rarely did.

"Prep the transports," was all he offered, to the small handful of officers waiting behind him. "We move out Sunday."

Nobody asked him to elaborate on what moving meant, exactly, or what he intended to bring with him when he did. Nobody, watching the particular stillness in his face, especially wanted to.

⁂

Deep inside the mountain that held it, behind every layer of armor and light anyone had thought to stack around it, the fragment sat exactly where it had sat for weeks now, doing exactly what it always did.

For one brief, unwitnessed moment, its glow deepened — the faintest possible shift, gone almost before it started, with no one present to see it happen at all.

Whether it was reacting to transports being readied somewhere close by, crates loaded, orders quietly given. Or whether it was reacting to something else entirely, something considerably further away and considerably harder to prepare for.

Nobody would ever be entirely sure which.

Chapter Twenty-Three — Oko

"Explain to me again," Mateo said, studying the trajectory plot with the specific intensity of a man solving a problem he'd already decided was more interesting than it strictly needed to be, "why we can't just call from where we already are."

"Twice in the same decade," Providence said, the moment the connection stabilized. "I must be getting popular in my old age."

"Provi."

The word came out right, the cadence exactly the same as always. Providence, unfortunately, had never once needed the cadence to tell him what he needed to know.

"You sound like yourself," he said, gentle. "You don't sound like yourself underneath it. What's going on, Oko?"

Mateo opened his mouth to deflect, the way he always deflected, and found, to his own considerable surprise, that the joke simply wasn't there to reach for this time.

"I don't know," he admitted, quieter than he meant to. "That's — that's actually the honest answer. I don't know."

He told him, then, in pieces, the thing he'd never quite said out loud to anyone, not even Elara, not in all these years. That he'd been some version of a believer for as long as he could remember being anything at all, with no family history to explain it, no five generations of careful, documented conviction behind him — just a fact of himself he'd never been able to trace back to a source. That he'd never doubted, not once, borrowing Elara's certainty when he couldn't manufacture his own. That somewhere in the last several months, alone with all of it in a way he hadn't been before, the not-doubting had started to feel less like faith and more like exhaustion, and he genuinely didn't know anymore whether he was doing right by any of the choices that had led him here.

"I'm tired, Provi," he said. "I don't know if I'm tired of the right things. That's the part I can't work out."

Providence let that sit a long moment before he answered.

"Oko," he said, gently. "Join me."

⁂

They breathed together — three long, deliberate breaths, eyes closed on both ends of a connection that spanned considerably more distance than either of them usually bothered acknowledging.

"What do you see?" Providence asked.

"I see what I always see." Mateo's voice was very quiet now. "All green."

"Why?"

He didn't have an answer ready. He searched for one anyway, the way you search a room you already suspect is empty.

"I don't know anymore."

"Oko. Look at me."

Mateo opened his eyes.

Providence had never once shown himself fully — not in all their years of calls, not even close. What sat across the connection now was no longer a voice alone, no longer even a suggestion of a shape. It was something closer to what Kael had once been to a mess hall full of strangers — vast, green-tinged, unmistakably present in a way that costume or distance could no longer soften. Not spectacle. Not performance. Simply, finally, the truth of him, offered without ceremony, because Mateo had finally asked the kind of question that earned it.

"Be patient," Providence said, and there was nothing more to it than that — no explanation, no resolution, just the plain, unhurried instruction of someone who had watched a great many people arrive exactly where Mateo currently stood, and knew, with total certainty, that patience was the only true answer any of them had ever needed.

⁂

"Always a pleasure, Provi," Mateo said, holding his voice together through sheer, practiced will.

"Always, Oko."

He cut the transmission himself this time, quickly, before either of them could draw it out the way they usually did.

The bridge sat empty and silent around him. He made it exactly as far as the pilot's chair before whatever he'd been holding together for the entire call finally gave way completely — no sound at first, just his shoulders folding inward, his hands pressed hard against his own face, five years and considerably longer than that finally arriving all at once, with nowhere left to go but out.

Nobody was there to see it. He wouldn't have wanted anyone to be.

Chapter Twenty-Four — What the Fragment Remembers

The requisition form listed it, in the flat, careful language of official inventory, as one (1) fragment, composition unknown, coloration: obsidian.

The PFC looked at the fragment one more time, decided the color of a rock several pay grades above his concern, and signed.

He didn't mention it again. He also didn't stop noticing it, the whole ride out — the faint, stubborn green that seemed to deepen, ever so slightly, the further they traveled from the base and the closer they got to wherever they were actually delivering it.

⁂

The church was mid-song when the sound reached them — a low, distant rumble that built, over several long minutes, into an unmistakable convoy, engines and rotors and the particular authoritative weight of vehicles that had never once needed to announce themselves quietly.

He didn't take her. He didn't need to, not yet. He'd seen exactly what he'd come to see.

"We'll see you again soon," Elara promised, more than one hand held a beat longer than usual on her way out.

Ross hadn't slept properly in three days.

"It's not slowing down," he told HANNAH, staring at a scroll of chatter that had, in the last seventy-two hours alone, outpaced everything the last five years combined had produced. "It's not even close to slowing down."

"No," HANNAH agreed. "It's accelerating."

Beyond his small basement rig, across the wider net entirely, the same acceleration played out in a thousand smaller ways — ordinary people, with no equipment and no history of caring, suddenly finding themselves unable to look away, lighting their own small, undisciplined fires of speculation and outrage across every platform built to carry them.

Two wildfires, still. Neither one slowing down.

⁂

Lira slept the whole shuttle ride back to LEO, curled against Elara's side, exhausted in the particular, uncomplicated way of a child who'd spent an entire day being loved by more people than she'd ever met in one place.

The Horizon Echo welcomed them back the way it always did — Mateo waiting at the airlock with an expression that suggested he'd cried recently and had no intention of discussing it, Kita already winding figure-eights around everyone's ankles before the hatch had even fully cycled.

"Why are we up so high?" she asked, to no one in particular.

Nobody in the room had an answer that felt like the whole truth. Everyone in the room, quietly, already suspected they were about to need one.

Chapter Twenty-Five — Kael's Intention

Kael surfaced from whatever passed, for him, as rest, and found the day's first notation already waiting.

Even faster than expected, again, he thought, and something in the observation pleased him considerably more than it concerned him. He allowed himself a small, private smile, rising from the low hovering seat into a slow, unhurried stretch.

On the bridge, the familiar path loaded the way it always loaded — and then, past the point where Earth itself sat waiting, simply didn't continue at all. No further plan. No projected line beyond it. No color, no shape, nothing at all where a whole galaxy's worth of future usually unspooled itself in patient, orderly detail.

He studied the blank space for a long moment, turning it over the way you'd turn over a genuinely interesting puzzle rather than a warning.

How curious, he thought, and found that he meant it entirely. Not everything needed to be alarming simply because it was unprecedented. Some things were only ever going to be interesting, right up until the moment they finally explained themselves.

He hummed something old and half-remembered under his breath the whole way there, unbothered, unhurried, exactly as certain of himself as he'd ever been.

⁂

"So," Mateo said, over a plate of something that only loosely resembled breakfast, "what happened while I was down there?"

"You were in jail, Mateo. That's what happened."

"I was detained, briefly, on a technicality that never actually resulted in charges. There's a difference, and the difference is doing a lot of work for my self-image right now."

Elara allowed herself a small, genuine laugh, the first one that had felt entirely uncomplicated in longer than she wanted to examine.

"I keep questioning myself," she admitted, after a while, turning her cup slowly between her hands. "Every step of this. Whether I'm doing right by her. I don't think I've stopped questioning it once, honestly, not since the very beginning."

"But you don't doubt it."

"No." She said it simply, the way she said most things she'd actually settled. "I question. I don't doubt. I don't think those are the same thing, even though most people treat them like they are."

Mateo was quiet a moment, turning something over of his own.

"I don't doubt it either," he said finally. "Not anymore. I used to borrow that from you, a little, if I'm honest. I'm not doing that anymore."

She looked at him carefully. "What changed?"

He didn't answer that directly, and something in his face made clear he wasn't going to, not today, maybe not ever — a private thing, settled somewhere she wasn't owed access to, and she found, somewhat to her own surprise, that she didn't need to press for it.

"Why were we up so high, by the way," she asked instead, letting the other question go. "Last week. The altitude."

"Had to call an old friend," Mateo said, simply, and left it exactly there.

Before she could push further, the door burst open with considerably more force than any door in that hallway was designed to withstand, Kita skidding in first at full, uncoordinated sprint, Lira right behind him, the fragment clutched in both hands and glowing brighter than either of them had ever seen it hold.

"I think he's coming," she said, breathless, eyes wide with something too large for the room to properly contain. "I think he's coming!"

Chapter Twenty-Six — Cameras and Weapons

Ross hadn't left the basement in two days. He'd stopped pretending he might.

"Confirm it again," he said, and HANNAH, patient as ever, confirmed it again — the same inbound signature, closing fast, unmistakably him.

⁂

Across the wider world, the two fires that had been burning separately for weeks finally found each other.

Church steps overflowed onto lawns, onto streets, onto whatever open ground could hold another body — the quiet, hand-to-hand belief wildfire finally catching up to and merging with the loud, headline-driven panic that had been consuming every feed for a month. Somewhere else, riots. Curfews. Cities locking down in ninety-six-hour windows nobody fully explained. It was, all of it, happening everywhere, all at once, in every direction a person could look.

⁂

Olivia logged the same handful of readings she'd been logging for days, calm as ever, and forwarded them along exactly the way she always did.

⁂

The General reviewed his own preparations one final time — coordinates confirmed, timing confirmed, a single ship in low orbit that had, by his own careful reasoning, become the only target that mattered.

"The Echo," he said, to no one in particular, and left it at that.

⁂

Elara's own small church stood full to its doors that morning, warm and certain, exactly as it had been the last several times she'd visited. Across the planet, in buildings of every shape and size and denomination, the same warmth repeated itself, over and over, an entire species finding, all at once, considerably more comfort in each other than it had in a very long time.

⁂

Night fell, and with it came spotlights unseen in generations, flooding skies that should have carried drones and personal aircraft and carried, instead, only searching light and an eerie, held-breath stillness.

⁂

The alert reached Olivia's desk one final time.

"Confirmed inbound, no threat classification warranted," she noted, the same flat, steady language she'd used every time before, though her hands, for the first time in longer than she could remember, weren't quite as steady as her words.

Above her, far beyond anything her own console could show her, Kael's ship settled into formation beside the Horizon Echo, obsidian and unhurried.

This time, the whole planet was watching.

Chapter Twenty-Seven — Departure

Every screen on Earth carried some version of it — television, tablet, livestream, a dozen amateur feeds hastily patched together, satellite downlinks never meant for public consumption suddenly wide open to anyone who knew where to look.

Ross watched with both hands flat on the console, not touching anything, for once simply letting it happen in front of him.

Olivia watched from her office, alone, the readout steady in front of her and her own hands considerably less steady than the readout.

The General watched from a command room full of quiet, waiting people, coordinates already locked, timing already set, watching the way a man watches the last few seconds before he commits to something he's decided he can no longer walk back from.

Congregations across the planet watched together, in crowded rooms and overflowing lawns, hands found and held without anyone quite deciding to reach first.

And aboard the Horizon Echo, Kael arrived on the bridge the way he always arrived — without transition, without fanfare, simply present.

"I heard you ran into an old friend of mine," Kael said, and something in the easy warmth of it confirmed, without needing to say so directly, exactly who that friend had been.

Kael smiled and spoke before Mateo could respond. "He speaks very highly of you."

Lira arrived at a dead run, and their embrace, when it landed, filled the whole bridge with light — not heat, not anything that scorched or threatened, simply brightness, total and complete, the way a room floods with sun the instant a curtain's finally pulled back. Kita wound in around both their ankles, purring loud enough to be heard even over everything else.

⁂

Kael and Elara moved to the Vigil together, and settled into the same ritual posture they'd shared once before, years and a lifetime ago.

"Please, join me," he said, and she did.

What passed between them there, nobody else would ever hear. No dialogue would ever surface from that room, no explanation, no answer to any of the questions the rest of the world was currently screaming into every available feed. Whatever needed saying, it seemed, could be said in considerably less than words.

⁂

Down the corridor, in the small common room, Lira had a fresh deck spread across the low table, doing her level best to teach Kita and Mateo a game with rules that seemed, even by her own account, to change slightly every time she explained them.

"You have to pick," she told Kita, patient and exasperated in exactly the way she'd been years ago, teaching him the exact same lesson on an entirely different afternoon.

Kita considered the spread of cards with tremendous, unhurried seriousness, then extended one paw directly toward Mateo, tapping once against his knee.

"He wants me to pick for him," Mateo said, delighted, scooping up the indicated card with all the ceremony of a man handling a genuine diplomatic request. "He's very clear about it. Grab his, specifically. Not yours. His."

"That's not how the game works, Mateo."

"It's exactly how the game works. He's using an intermediary. That's advanced strategy."

Kita, entirely unbothered by the debate happening on his behalf, settled deeper into the cushion and began, with tremendous dignity, to groom one paw.

⁂

"We're ready," Elara said, once she and Kael had risen from the Vigil, and something in the way she said it carried both total confidence and the smallest thread of genuine question underneath. "Are we?"

"Yes," Kael said. "We are ready." A pause, and something crossed his face that looked, for the first time since he'd arrived, almost like uncertainty of his own — not doubt, exactly, but the honest, unguarded acknowledgment that even he, who had done this more times than he easily counted, didn't fully know what came next either.

The farewells came quickly after that, each one carrying the same steady, unshaken register.

Lira held him a long moment, her glow brighter than it had ever been, not one thread of sadness in the whole embrace. Mateo shook his hand — an actual, solid handshake, warm and sure — with nothing in his face but total confidence. And Elara met his eyes one final time, her own bright with tears she made no effort to hide, his glow blazing brighter than she'd ever seen it hold.

He faded from the bridge the way he always faded — without ceremony, without a countdown, simply here, and then, gently, elsewhere.

⁂

The last anyone saw of him was in his own quarters, walls given over entirely to blank cosmic background, no path loaded anywhere across them, no plan, no line, no guide. He settled into the low hovering seat, tapping one hand idly against his knee, rocking, very slightly, to a rhythm only he could hear.

His vessel began to fold itself away from view.

Below, on the small, blue, endlessly loud planet he'd just said goodbye to, the first flashes had already begun — green, and then white, scattered here and there across the dark curve of the Earth.

Some worlds had already started their own Shift.

Others had already started launching.

Chapter Twenty-Eight — Containment

The alarms hit the Echo's bridge before Kael's ship had even fully faded from view — Eliza and Reggie both talking over each other, listing possible launch sites faster than either of them could confirm them, the room filling, all at once, with a kind of fear neither Elara nor Mateo had ever needed to actually feel together before.

Mateo turned to her, and there was nothing in his face but total, unshaken readiness.

"Orders, Captain?"

⁂

Ross had fallen asleep at his console sometime after midnight, headphones still on, and woke to a sound he'd never once heard his rig produce in ten years of listening to it — every channel, every band, screaming at once.

"HANNAH—"

"I know, Ross. I'm already tracking it."

Olivia's desktop paused mid-task, the cursor freezing over a routine field she'd been filling in without much thought at all, and went dark.

SEEK SHELTER IMMEDIATELY.

Every missile built for maximum damage across the planet had already launched — not from any single order, not from any one nation's deliberate choice, but from a cascade that had been building the instant Kael's ship registered, across a dozen aging legacy defense networks, as an anomaly too significant for their oldest, most brittle protocols to process safely. One system flagged it. The next system trusted the first. The one after that trusted both. By the time any human hand reached for a switch, the switch had already been thrown, a thousand times over, by machines that had simply been doing exactly what they were built to do a little too well.

Every feed that had been carrying Kael's departure live, across every screen on Earth, cut instead to the same flat, urgent warning tone.

The General's own command room had emptied around him sometime in the last several minutes — officers, aides, everyone who'd had anywhere safer to be, gone. He launched.

A single, brief flash of green filled the whole of the visible cosmos.

Chapter Twenty-Nine — Earth's Shift

The first missile disappeared mid-arc, high above the northern hemisphere, swallowed whole in a bloom of green light that left behind no debris, no shockwave, no sound at all — one moment a slender, deadly shape cutting across a night sky, the next, simply nothing, as though the sky itself had reached up and quietly erased a mistake.

Three more went the same way within seconds of each other, in three different corners of the world, each capture identical in its total, silent completeness. No fire. No fragments. No trace that anything dangerous had ever existed in that particular stretch of air at all.

A pair of smaller detonations, closer to the ground, met a similar fate — caught at the very last possible instant, contained in spheres of green-white light that held for a heartbeat before folding in on themselves and vanishing, taking whatever damage they'd have caused along with them.

The mercy extended even to the people who least deserved to receive it.

A soft, curved dome of the same light settled over Ross's small house, holding steady, translucent, letting him watch the whole unfolding sky through it without so much as a flicker of static disturbing his signal. Another rose over Olivia's building, the entire structure suddenly cocooned in something that looked, from a distance, almost like a soap bubble caught in low morning light. And in the General's own command bunker — abandoned by everyone but him, the man who'd just launched the only attack still incoming from anywhere on the planet — the same gentle barrier rose without hesitation or judgment, protecting him precisely as thoroughly as it protected anyone else.

⁂

The largest detonation came where the largest detonations always eventually did — a full, designed strike, landing exactly as intended, directly over the heart of a mid-sized coastal city.

From a hillside several miles out, a lone figure filming on a handheld device caught the whole thing in a single unbroken shot: the first blinding flash, white and total, swallowing three square miles of skyline in an instant. The fireball climbed, boiling upward into the unmistakable shape a species had spent the better part of a century learning to dread on sight, debris and light and heat rolling outward in a wave that should have, by any law anyone understood, reached that hillside within seconds and ended the recording along with everything else in its path.

The shockwave never arrived.

A heartbeat before the sound could catch up to the light, a second flash bloomed outward from the city's own center — green, this time, vast and sudden, swallowing the entire spreading wave of heat and force in one impossible instant. When it faded, the skyline stood exactly as it had before, windows intact, streets empty but whole, ash drifting down onto pavement that had never actually cracked at all.

Somewhere in that city, people who had felt the exact moment they should have died were, instead, simply still standing, blinking in disbelief at a sky that had gone, without any warning at all, entirely and impossibly quiet.

⁂

Aboard the Horizon Echo, Elara and Mateo stood side by side at the viewport, watching the whole of it unfold across the curve of the Earth below — bursts of green swallowing missiles whole, slower blooms of white folding into green wherever a detonation had already begun, small, subtle pulses of light rising and fading in places too quiet and too numerous to count, each one, somewhere, a single life quietly choosing coherence over collapse.

Neither of them said anything for a long time. It felt, standing there, like finally occupying the exact seat Kael himself had sat in for every one of the thirteen worlds that had come before this one — the answer to a question the two of them had been circling since the very first briefing, all those years ago, in a much smaller, much less certain version of this same room.

Mateo noticed it first — a faint warmth at the edge of his own vision, and when he looked down at his hands, a thin, unmistakable thread of green light tracing beneath his skin.

He looked at Elara.

She was already looking back, her own eyes catching the exact same low, impossible glow.

"Impossible," they said, together, at exactly the same moment, in exactly the same disbelieving breath.

They moved toward each other without deciding to, the way you move toward something you don't yet have language for, the distance between them closing slowly, uncertainly, neither of them entirely sure what came next or what this strange, shared light actually meant for either of them.

They were close enough to touch when the word arrived behind them, quiet and entirely unafraid.

"Mom."

They turned.

Lira stood in the doorway, small and steady, more confident than either of them had ever seen her — her skin carrying the same deep, unmistakable obsidian quality they'd only ever seen on one other person, threaded through with the faintest, familiar shimmer of green and blue.

Chapter Thirty — Lira Becomes

"Lira!"

Elara crossed the room in an instant, one hand still reaching back for Mateo's, pulling him into the embrace right alongside her — the three of them folded together, Lira's new, strange, beautiful skin warm against both their arms, nothing about the moment feeling anything less than exactly, entirely right.

"We're okay."

⁂

Fewer white flashes rose to meet green ones now. Fewer green blooms answered anything at all. The great, planet-wide brightness that had filled the whole curve of the Earth only an hour before began, gently, to dim — not fading into darkness, exactly, but into something quieter, steadier, the low, patient hum of a world that had just done something enormous and was, for the first time in longer than anyone could remember, finally still.

"What does that mean," Mateo said, quiet, almost to himself, "for a species that's spent its whole history trying to be the loudest thing in the room?"

⁂

Mateo's had done the same.

"Lira—"

Lira considered the question with real seriousness, the way she considered most things, and then broke into a grin too wide and too delighted for the moment to hold any real weight at all.

"This is so cool," she said.

Chapter Thirty-One — What Elara Chose

Lira had generated, over the course of one quiet afternoon, an entire assortment of cat toys — a feather on a string, a small mechanical mouse with erratic, unpredictable movement, a simple ball that did nothing but roll — and was conducting, with total scientific seriousness, a thorough survey of Kita's actual preferences among them.

"He likes the feather," she announced, "but he respects the mouse."

"That's an interesting distinction," Mateo said, watching from the low bench beside Elara, both of them content, for once, to simply exist in a moment that asked absolutely nothing else of them.

It was there, in that small, ordinary quiet, that he noticed the tear.

It slid slowly down Elara's cheek, unhurried, and she made no move to wipe it away or explain it, her eyes fixed on her daughter with an expression that carried the whole weight of something enormous, something that had clearly been building for longer than this single moment — six generations of reaching for a question nobody in her family had ever quite finished asking. Stay here, on this ground her people had worked and loved and buried each other in for over a century. Or go wherever there actually was, the place her whole family had always quietly believed they were meant for, all along.

Mateo didn't ask what the tear meant. He understood, watching her, that some decisions didn't need to be spoken to already be final.

"Elara."

She turned to him, and the tear caught the light as it moved — an unmistakable, familiar glow, the exact color he'd seen once before in a five-year-old's veins and had never expected to see anywhere else again.

He reached out, gently, and wiped it from her cheek.

His own hand, when he drew it back, carried the faintest trace of that same light along his fingers — and when Elara looked up at him properly, she found his eyes had changed entirely, gone the same deep, unmistakable obsidian she'd only ever seen on two people in her whole life.

"Wonderful," he said, quiet, entirely unafraid, the same word Kael had once given Lira, the same word Elara had once given him, passed now, one more time, into whatever came next for both of them.

Two fates, decided in the very same breath, while the third sat cross-legged on the floor a few feet away, entirely unaware that anything at all had just changed.

⁂

The farm looked the same from the air as it always had — wind turbines turning slow along the ridgeline, six generations of careful, stubborn work folded into every acre of it. Elara walked the property line that evening the way she'd walked it a thousand times before, and found, for the first time in longer than she could name, that she wasn't waiting for anything else to happen. She had already arrived, precisely, exactly where she'd always actually been headed.

⁂

Aboard the Horizon Echo, Mateo settled into the captain's chair with an ice cream cone in one hand and an expression of tremendous, unearned self-satisfaction.

"I am the captain now," he announced, to Reggie and Eliza both, the only crew he currently had and, for the time being, entirely enough.

"That is not how rank works," Reggie said.

"It's exactly how rank works. I said it with confidence. That's the whole system."

⁂

In the small, quiet room that held the Vigil, Lira settled into the low, hovering seat her mother had once occupied every morning of her life — the exact posture Kael himself had once folded into, unhurried and entirely at peace, facing the same small, careful arrangement of science and family and belief that had started all of this, six generations and a lifetime ago.

Far beyond anything any instrument could properly measure, Kael drifted through his own quiet stretch of the cosmos, tapping one hand idly against his knee, rocking, very slightly, to a rhythm only he could hear, dissolving, patiently, into everything.

Back in the small room, Lira closed her eyes.

Kita settled beside her, in the exact spot he'd claimed for himself years ago, and turned his face up toward hers.

His eyes caught the low light — bright, impossibly green, tiny stars flickering somewhere in their depths — and slowly, unhurried, entirely at peace, he blinked.

I am not afraid of you, and I would like you to know it.
